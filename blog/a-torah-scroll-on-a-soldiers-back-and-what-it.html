A Torah scroll on a soldier's back, and what it taught me about business.

By Tal Paperin· April 14, 2026· 5 min read
Revenue LeadershipB2B SalesNegotiation

Heading into Lebanon.

This is what fighting for something real looks like.

Not a slogan. Not a hashtag. Not a press release.

A soldier who decided that even in the middle of war, even walking toward the darkest places on earth, he carries light with him. A Torah scroll, on his back.

That's the difference between people who stand for something and people who just stand.

And it's a distinction I think about in business every single day.

The darkness in the boardroom

Because the world is full of darkness in the boardroom too.

  • Sales teams that lie to close deals.
  • Consultants who sell frameworks they've never actually executed.
  • "Partners" who shake your hand and stab you in the back.
  • Leaders who say whatever the room wants to hear.

I've seen all of it. Up close.

And I've built my entire career on the opposite.

Almost none of these people think of themselves as dishonest, and that is worth understanding, because it is how the habit gets into your own company. The rep who promises a feature that isn't built tells himself engineering will catch up. The leader who tells the board what it wants to hear calls it managing the room. Each one is a small convenience taken under pressure from a number. Stack enough of them and you have a company where nobody knows what is true anymore.

You win by being true

You don't win long-term by being clever. You win by being true.

True about what your product can and can't do. True about what the client actually needs, even when it costs you the deal. True about the hard conversations that most salespeople avoid.

I mean this as a commercial argument, and here is how it works.

B2B revenue is built on what happens after the signature. The customer renews, buys more, and tells other people. A deal closed on an exaggeration gets none of that. The customer discovers the gap during onboarding, your delivery team absorbs his anger, and he leaves at the first opportunity.

This is exactly what I fix, hands-on. Monthly, no contract, no exit fines. If revenue is stuck, tell me where.

Let's Talk

The honest deal can be slower to close and smaller on day one. It renews.

There is an internal side as well. A salesperson who bends the truth with customers bends it with you. The habit that tells a buyer "yes, we do that" is the same one that tells a manager "yes, it will close this month." Your forecast ends up built from the same material as his promises, which is a large part of why sales forecasts are always wrong.

Here is what each of those looks like inside a sales team.

True about what the product can and can't do. Every rep should be able to say "we don't do that" without flinching. Write down what you don't do and who you are not for, and hand it to the team. A buyer who hears a clear no on one point believes your yes on the others.

True about what the client actually needs, even when it costs you the deal. Sometimes discovery shows that the client needs something else, or something smaller, or nothing yet. Say so. You lose that deal and keep a person who trusts you. People change companies, and they remember who was straight with them.

True about the hard conversations. Price. The decision maker who has not appeared yet. The delivery date you cannot meet. The point on which a competitor is better. Most salespeople postpone these to protect the mood of the meeting, and the deal dies later, quietly, for exactly those reasons. Raise them early, while something can still be done.

How to tell if your company has a truth problem

  • New customers are surprised during onboarding by what the product does not do.
  • Customers leave in the first year and say it was "not what we expected."
  • Roadmap items are being sold as if they exist.
  • Bad news reaches you late and already softened.

That last sign belongs to the leader. People bring you the truth at the speed you have taught them is safe.

What to do this week

  1. Ask your delivery or customer success people which promises they most often have to walk back. Fix those in the pitch first.
  2. Write the "we don't do this, and we are not for you if" page together with the sales team.
  3. In the next pipeline review, ask each rep for one deal that should come out.
  4. Look at who you hire and what you reward. If only the signature is rewarded and nobody checks whether the customer stayed, you are paying for the behavior you say you don't want. Getting this right from the first hire is part of building a sales team properly.

None of this is a one-time workshop. Holding a team to that standard week after week is plain management work, and it is a big part of what I do as a fractional CRO.

"Honesty costs deals"

Yes, it does. It costs you the deals that would have cost you more later: the customer who leaves angry, the refund, the reference you can never use, and a team that learned exaggeration is how things are done here.

The harder version of the objection is "my competitors lie, and they are winning." Some of them are, this quarter. Clever wins quarters. I wrote "long-term" on purpose.

The same holds at the negotiating table. When the other side discovers one inflated claim, every number you gave becomes suspect and the price conversation starts over. When everything you said checks out, you have very little left to defend.

Light doesn't negotiate with darkness. It just shows up, and the darkness disappears. In a market full of inflated claims, the vendor who says exactly what is true is easy to spot.

That soldier isn't carrying a Torah because it's easy. He's carrying it because some things are worth protecting no matter the cost.

I carry that same principle into every engagement.
