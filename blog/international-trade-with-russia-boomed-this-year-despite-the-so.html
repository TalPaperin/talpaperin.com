International trade with Russia boomed this year, despite the so-called "sanctions" many countries imposed after the Ukraine invasion.

Countries vowed to sever economic ties with Russia and imposed sanctions that were intended to cripple its economy after it invaded Ukraine. But as one of the world's most important producers of oil, gas and raw materials, Russia has had longstanding and lucrative trade partnerships. Breaking those ties is not easy.

Though the volume of its imports has plunged as sanctions and trade limits went into effect, a few countries, including China and Turkey, have deepened their relationships with Russia since the war began. That has led to a frustrating reality for Western officials who had hoped to undercut Russia's war effort by punishing its economy: the value of its exports actually grew after it invaded Ukraine.

So what's next?

Many analysts claim that Russia's ample trade, and the war chest it has generated, could start to dwindle in the next year as more sanctions bite.

Will it?

As long as the major players (China, India, Turkey and others) remain outside the sanctions game, it will NOT.

Why I put "sanctions" in quotation marks

I look at this as someone who has spent 20-plus years selling across borders. The politics I leave to others. What interests me is the commercial mechanics, because the same mechanics decide whether your own market entry works.

A sanction is a decision by one side to stop trading with the other. It hurts the target only if the target cannot replace the buyers and suppliers who left. Russia sells oil, gas and raw materials, things the world consumes every day. A buyer who needs them and is not bound by the sanctions has every reason to keep buying.

So when some customers walk away and the product is still needed, the trade does not stop. It reroutes. The evidence is in the story itself: China and Turkey deepened their relationships, and the value of exports went up.

A boycott that the biggest buyers do not join is an announcement.

Volume and value are two different numbers

Look at the two figures side by side. The volume of Russia's imports plunged. The value of its exports grew. Both are true at the same time, and each side of the argument quotes the one that suits it.

CEOs do the same thing with their own business every quarter. One number says the market is collapsing and another says it is a record year. Before you conclude anything about a market, ask which number you are being shown and who chose it. Imports or exports. Volume or value. Signed contracts or cash in the bank. I make the same point about pipeline numbers in my guide to sales pipeline and forecasting: a total with no definition behind it tells you nothing.

What this has to do with your company

Here is what I take from it as an operator.

Declarations are not demand. Governments declared the ties severed, and the trade data says otherwise. The version of this in your market is the buyer who tells you "we are definitely moving away from our current vendor" and then renews with them. Watch what people purchase. A purchase order is the only vote that counts.

The biggest players decide the outcome. Whether these sanctions bite depends on a handful of large buyers, and each one acts on its own interest. Every market you enter works this way. A few large buyers or distributors set the rules, and their incentives tell you more than any forecast does. Before you enter a country, find out who they are and what they gain or lose if you succeed.

Old trade relationships are stubborn. "Longstanding and lucrative trade partnerships. Breaking those ties is not easy." For anyone who sells B2B, that is the most useful line in the whole story. Your prospect's relationship with his current supplier is longstanding, and it is lucrative for someone. A better brochure will not break it. You need a reason strong enough to make switching worth the pain, and you need to know who inside the buyer's company benefits from leaving things as they are.

Five checks before you commit budget to a new market

I run these before any market entry plan gets money behind it:

  1. Who buys this category there today, and from whom? Get it from distributors and customers, the people who move the goods. Announcements don't count.
  2. Which few players control most of the buying, and what does each of them want?
  3. What would make them switch supplier, and what does switching cost them?
  4. Which number have you been shown, volume or value, and what does the other one say?
  5. Which assumption in your plan requires someone to act against his own interest? Remove it and see if the plan still stands.

If you are an Israeli company looking west, the same checks sit at the start of my guide to US market entry. And they belong in the first week of any serious go-to-market strategy, before anyone books a flight.

"The analysts say it will change next year"

Maybe it will. The analysts say the trade and the war chest could start to dwindle as more sanctions bite, and I notice the word "could."

A forecast that depends on new measures working better than the old ones is a hope. My read is simpler. The money keeps flowing for as long as the large buyers stay outside the game. If one of them changes position, the picture changes with it. That is the variable to watch, and nothing in the announcements tells you about it.

It is the same in your business. Forecasts, reports and declarations describe what people say they will do. Trade follows the buyers who have a reason to buy. So will it dwindle? As long as China, India, Turkey and the others stay out, it will not.
