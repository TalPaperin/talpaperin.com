Here's MY data from the past week.

I started going through my old Calendly meetings from the last 3 years. Mostly startup founders, CEOs and VP Sales, about 300 of them. One by one I started Googling the companies and checking their LinkedIns.

So far around 25% are completely gone (and I've covered 200+). Another 10% replaced leadership, replaced the VP Sales or completely changed direction.

This is not a study. It is one operator's calendar, and everyone on it had booked a sales call with me, so it isn't a random sample. Read it as a field note. But after reading all the shutdown posts, the news articles and the "we ran out of runway" explanations, the same patterns keep showing up again and again.

## They did not die of bad technology

Most of them did NOT fail because the technology, or the product in general, was bad.

Most of them failed because they spent years building product, hiring developers, opening offices, raising money, adding features and talking about vision, while sales and go-to-market were treated like something they would "figure out later." Or it would happen by itself at some point. Somehow.

Later came.

And the market didn't care.

The brutal part is that many of those products were actually good. Some were VERY good.

But good products without strong commercial execution, with some heavy planning in advance, die every single day.

## Why "later" feels reasonable

No founder decides to neglect sales. It happens one sensible decision at a time.

Product work feels like progress. You ship a feature, you can see it, you can demo it to investors. Selling feels like rejection, and the results are slow and messy. Give a technical founder a free week and it goes to the roadmap. Every time.

Then there is the false comfort of early revenue. The first customers come from the founder's network, a former employer, an investor's introduction. Those deals close because people trust the founder personally. They say very little about whether a stranger will buy. The company reads them as proof the product sells, and builds for another year on that belief. I wrote about where that road ends in [founder-led sales won't scale](/founder-led-sales-wont-scale).

And "runway" hides the clock. A sales motion takes time to build even when everything goes right. You have to find the buyer, learn the message, hire, ramp and get through a full sales cycle before you know whether it works. A company that starts that work with a few months of cash left is already out of time. Nobody has told it yet.

## Signs you are on the list

Check yourself against these.

- Engineering headcount keeps growing and nobody in the company owns a revenue number.
- Your pipeline lives in the founder's inbox and memory.
- Your paying customers all came through someone you already knew.
- You have a product roadmap for the next 18 months and no written plan for who you will sell to next quarter.
- You describe traction in pilots, signups, demos and "strong interest."
- The plan is to hire a head of sales after the next round.

This matters more now, when everybody has AI, everybody has automation, everybody has dashboards and everybody claims they are revolutionary. A buyer who hears the same claim from ten vendors stops listening to claims and buys from whoever shows up, understands the problem and follows through. That is commercial execution, and no feature replaces it.

## What to do this quarter

1. **Put a name on revenue.** One person owns the number. If it has to be the founder for now, fine, but then it gets real hours in the calendar every week, protected the way you protect a product release.
2. **Sell to strangers.** Pick a narrow target, write down who the buyer is and what hurts, and get meetings with people who have never heard of you. Their reaction is the truth about your market. If you have no way to reach them, that is a problem you can pay to solve, [per qualified meeting](/services/pay-per-meeting), without building a team first.
3. **Count money.** Track signed contracts and cash collected. Everything before that is a hypothesis.
4. **Write the plan before you need it.** Who you sell to, through which channel, at what price, with what message, and what it costs to win a customer. A [go-to-market strategy](/services/go-to-market-strategy) on five honest pages beats a vision deck.
5. **Do it while there is still money.** Count backward from the end of your runway. Subtract the time it takes to build a motion and close a deal in your market. The date you land on is your real deadline, and for a lot of founders it has already passed.

## "We're not ready to sell yet"

I hear this constantly. The product needs two more features. The UI isn't finished. One more quarter.

There's a real point inside it: you shouldn't scale the selling of a product that doesn't work. But selling early is how you find out what "works" means to someone who has to pay for it. Ten buyer conversations will reorder your roadmap faster than a year of internal debate, and they cost almost nothing. Waiting until the product is ready assumes you already know what the market wants. The companies on my list assumed that too.

I've been seeing this for 20 years across different industries and companies. Founders constantly underestimate how hard it is to actually make people buy.

Not to click. Not to like. Not to "show interest."

BUY.

And by the time many companies finally realize they have a sales, positioning or go-to-market problem, they have already burned most of the money.
