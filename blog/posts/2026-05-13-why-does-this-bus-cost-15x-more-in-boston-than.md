Same size, same purpose, same number of passengers.

One costs about $80,000. The other can hit $1.2 million.

The American bus is not 15x better. Not even close. Its lifespan is maybe 2x longer.

The gap has nothing to do with the bus itself. It comes from the system around the bus.

The more complex the system becomes, the more expensive everything inside it gets.

More procurement layers. More regulation. More management. More approvals. More compliance. More consultants. More overhead. More stakeholders.

Until eventually the system itself becomes more expensive than the actual product.

## Your sales org is the Boston bus

A lot of companies operate exactly like this now.

I walk into sales organizations where:

- the CRM costs more than the pipeline it helps manage
- 14 people are involved in approving discounts
- sales reps spend more time feeding dashboards than speaking to buyers
- founders pay enterprise pricing for mid-market outcomes

The craziest part is that bloated systems rarely look broken.

Everything appears impressive: expensive software, giant vendor contracts, reporting structures, weekly alignment calls, layers of management.

The bus still moves. That's why nobody questions it.

Until growth slows down. Margins collapse. Sales cycles stretch. And suddenly the company needs layoffs despite record spending.

## How the tax gets built

Nobody sets out to build a $1.2 million bus. Every layer was added by a reasonable person solving a real problem.

A rep gave away too much on one deal, so discounts now need a manager's approval. Then the finance lead wanted a look. Then legal. A forecast missed, so a new report was added, then a dashboard to track the report, then a weekly meeting to review the dashboard. A tool was bought to fix something the last tool didn't do, and the last tool stayed because someone still uses one screen of it.

Each decision made sense on the day. What nobody did was go back and remove anything. Adding a layer has an owner and a reason. Removing one has neither, and it carries a risk: if something goes wrong after you cut it, you are the one who cut it. So the layers only ever go in one direction.

The dangerous thing about complexity is that it compounds quietly. One extra layer sounds reasonable. Then five years later nobody remembers why half the layers exist.

And the cost lands in the worst possible place, which is selling time. Every approval step is a day the buyer waits. Every dashboard field is a minute the rep isn't talking to a customer. The software invoice is the visible part. The slow deals are the expensive part.

## Finding your system tax

Most founders are not paying for outcomes anymore. They're paying system tax.

And once you see it, you start seeing it everywhere.

$80K software solving the same problem as the $8K version.

$400K consultants producing PowerPoints an operator could explain in one whiteboard session.

Enterprise workflows built around protecting internal politics instead of serving customers.

You can measure yours in an afternoon. Four checks:

1. **Count the hands on a deal.** Take one recent deal and list every person who had to approve, review or sign something between the verbal yes and the signed contract. Next to each name, write how many days that step took.
2. **Ask your reps where the week goes.** Have each one estimate the hours spent with buyers against the hours spent on updates, reports and internal meetings. Don't argue with the numbers. Read them.
3. **Price the stack per seat.** Add up every sales tool, divide by the number of people who sell, and compare the result with what one rep closes in a month. The [sales cost calculator](/sales-cost-calculator) will give you the full cost of a seat, software and everything else.
4. **Open every report and ask who acted on it.** For each dashboard and weekly report, name the last decision that was made because of it. No decision, no report.

## Cutting it

Start with whatever sits between a buyer saying yes and a contract going out. That is where the tax costs the most.

Give reps a discount band they can use without asking anyone, and one named approver above it. Fourteen approvers don't produce better discipline than one. They produce a slower deal and a shared assumption that someone else checked.

Cancel the tools nobody would fight for. Announce that a tool will be switched off in two weeks and see who objects, and with what evidence.

Kill the meetings that exist to prepare for other meetings. Keep one pipeline review a week that runs straight off the CRM, built the way I lay out in the guide on [pipeline and forecasting](/sales-pipeline-and-forecasting).

Then set one rule for the future: nothing gets added unless something gets removed.

## "We're not a five-person startup. We need controls."

You do. The Boston bus does last longer, maybe 2x, so some of that process buys something real. A company with real revenue needs approvals, audit trails and reporting. I've built plenty of them.

So pay 2x for 2x. Then ask what the rest of the 15x is buying.

A control earns its place when you can name the specific loss it prevents, and that loss is bigger than the selling time it eats. Most layers fail that test the moment someone asks. Which is why nobody asks.

So ask. Go layer by layer and make each one justify its cost in deals and days. Most companies that do this discover they have been paying for the system and calling it growth.
