Then they use logic to explain what they already decided.

Trump gets this better than anyone.

One sentence. And suddenly everyone feels something. Rage, excitement, disbelief. Doesn't matter. He triggered a reaction.

You might hate him for it. Fine.

But if you don't understand what he's doing, you can't sell.

Your buyer isn't analyzing your deck. They're not reading your feature list. They're sitting across from you or skimming your email and they're feeling something. Or they're not.

Most of you are explaining.

"AI-powered." "End-to-end." "Scalable solution." "Revenue optimization."

Nothing. No feeling. No tension. No reason to care.

So they say "looks interesting" and you never hear from them again.

## Why "looks interesting" is the worst answer you can get

A "no" is useful. It tells you something: wrong buyer, wrong timing, wrong price. You can work with a no.

"Looks interesting" tells you the buyer felt nothing strong enough to argue with. They were polite, they closed the laptop, and your deal went into the pile of things that would be nice to look at someday. Someday has no budget.

Here is the mechanism inside a B2B purchase. Buying anything new is work and risk for the person across the table. They have to convince a boss, fight for budget, sit through procurement and put their name on the decision. Nobody volunteers for that because a slide said "scalable." They do it because something bothers them enough that doing nothing feels worse than doing the work.

That bother is the emotion. The business case, the ROI model and the feature comparison all come later, and their job is to help your buyer defend a decision already made in the gut. Logic is the paperwork.

## How to tell if your message is dead

I've watched brilliant founders lose deals they should have won. The product was strong. The message was dead. No emotion, no urgency, no relevance.

You can check this in your own pipeline this week. Look for these signs:

- First meetings go well and second meetings don't happen.
- Your CRM is full of deals stuck at the same early stage, with a last note that says "will get back to us."
- Prospects compliment the product and never mention a problem of their own.
- Your reps talk for most of the call.
- Nobody ever pushes back on price, because nobody gets far enough to care about price.

That last one surprises founders. A buyer who argues about price wants the thing. A buyer who doesn't argue about anything has already left.

If three of those describe your pipeline, more leads won't fix it. You will just kill more deals, faster. This is the usual story behind [reps who are busy and a pipeline that is empty](/hired-reps-but-no-pipeline).

## You don't need to be Trump

You don't need to be provocative or controversial. I'm not telling you to insult anyone.

You need to make your buyer feel something real. Pain. Urgency. Fear of falling behind. Desire to win.

That's the job. And it takes more discipline than it sounds, because the feeling has to be true. Manufactured urgency ("price goes up Friday") works once and costs you the relationship. Real urgency comes from the buyer's own situation, said back to them more clearly than they have said it themselves.

Compare two openings for the same product.

The first: "We're an AI-powered, end-to-end platform for revenue optimization."

The second: "Your forecast was wrong again last quarter, and you found out in the last two weeks, when it was too late to do anything about it."

The first describes you. The second describes their Tuesday. Only one of them gets a reaction.

## What to do about it this week

1. Take your last ten lost or stalled deals and write down, for each, what the buyer was afraid of or angry about. If you can't answer, your discovery is the problem. Your team is presenting before it understands anything.
2. Rewrite your first email and the first two minutes of your demo so they open with the buyer's problem in the buyer's words. Your product shows up after they nod.
3. Ban your own jargon. If a phrase could sit on any competitor's homepage, it carries no feeling. Cut it.
4. Change what you ask in discovery. "What happens if this isn't fixed by the end of the year?" does more work than twenty feature questions. Then stop talking and let them answer.
5. Give the buyer the logic afterward. Once they care, hand them the numbers they need to justify it to their CFO. Emotion gets the decision. Logic gets the signature.

## "My buyers are engineers. They're rational."

I hear this from technical founders all the time. Your buyers are engineers, finance people and procurement, and they run a scored evaluation with twelve criteria.

Sure. And who decided there would be an evaluation at all? Someone got frustrated enough, or scared enough, or ambitious enough to start one. The scoring sheet came after. Even the most analytical buyer has a boss to impress and a failure they don't want their name on.

Rational buyers have feelings about risk. Sell to that.

I've rebuilt messaging and sales motions for B2B companies across five continents. The pattern is always the same: great product, dead message, dying pipeline. It's where I start as a [Fractional CRO](/services/fractional-cro), and it's usually the first thing fixed in a [go-to-market rebuild](/services/go-to-market-strategy), before anyone hires another rep.

Your buyer has to feel something before they will think about you at all. Make that the test for every email, every deck and every first call.
