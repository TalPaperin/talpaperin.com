An Israeli drone company. A $982 million U.S. Army contract. JPMorgan leading the IPO.

But here's what the headline doesn't tell you.

UVision didn't plateau. They didn't stall. They didn't wonder why the pipeline dried up.

They knew exactly what they were selling, to whom, and why it mattered, and they scaled accordingly.

Most B2B companies I work with are the opposite. Good product. Working team. And revenue that hasn't moved in 6 months.

Leadership calls it a sales problem. What I usually find is a strategy problem dressed up as a sales problem.

## Why it gets misdiagnosed

It gets misdiagnosed because it shows up in the sales numbers. Fewer deals close, cycles stretch, the pipeline thins out. The sales team stands closest to the number, so the sales team gets the blame, then the training, then the new commission plan, and sometimes a new VP.

None of it works when the cause sits upstream. A rep can only work the list he is given, with the message he is given, on the buyer he was told to call. If those are off, extra effort just means more calls into the wrong place.

There is a simple way to tell the two apart. A sales problem is uneven: some reps hit their number and some don't, and deals are lost for different reasons. A strategy problem is even. Everybody is stuck in roughly the same way, including your best people.

## It is almost always one of these four

**Targeting the right industry but the wrong buyer inside it.** You picked the sector correctly. Inside each account, though, your team talks to whoever is easiest to reach, and that person cannot sign. You will recognize it by the friendly meetings, the champions who "need to take it upstairs," and the deals that die in a room you were never in.

**Positioning that sounds identical to every competitor.** Put your homepage headline next to those of your closest competitors and cover the logos. If you cannot tell which one is yours, the buyer can't either, and he will choose on price or on whoever he already knows. The telltale sign is a prospect asking "so how are you different?" late in the process.

**A sales motion built for early adopters that breaks on mainstream buyers.** Your first customers bought the founder's conviction and forgave what was missing. The next group wants references, proof and a clear process. The pitch that won the first group loses the second. Growth that was fast while the founder closed every deal, and flat once anyone else started selling, is the classic pattern. I cover it in [why founder-led sales won't scale](/founder-led-sales-wont-scale).

**No one actually owns the revenue strategy. Not really.** The CEO owns it in theory and has forty other things to do. The head of sales owns the quarter. Marketing owns leads. Nobody decides who you sell to, with what message and through which motion, and nobody is accountable when those don't fit together. Ask sales and marketing separately who your ideal customer is. Two different answers tell you the seat is empty.

## What UVision had that you don't

UVision had wars validate their product for them.

You don't get that luxury.

When a product is proven in real use, in front of the whole world, much of the selling job is already done. The buyer has seen what it does. Part of the clarity about what, to whom and why was handed to them by events.

For a B2B software or services company, nothing outside does that work for you. No event will prove your value. You have to build the same clarity on purpose: decide who the buyer is, produce your own proof through customer results and references, and explain why it matters in words a competitor could not copy onto his own website.

## A one-week test

Run this before you spend another dollar on leads or headcount.

1. Ask five people in your company (yourself, the head of sales, a rep, marketing, product) to write down separately who your buyer is and why he buys. Compare the answers. If you get five versions, you have found the problem.
2. Take your last ten wins. Which job title signed? What size of company? Look for the pattern you really win in, and compare it with the one your team is chasing.
3. Take ten stalled deals. Where did each one stop, and who on the buyer's side was missing from the conversation?
4. Read your pitch next to two competitors' pitches. Strike every sentence they could also say. See what is left.
5. Decide who owns the answers. One name.

Most of this is the first stage of a real [go-to-market strategy](/services/go-to-market-strategy). The rest is the willingness to act on what you find.

## "We just need more leads"

That is the first objection, and it is the expensive one. More leads poured into the wrong target with a generic message produce more stalled deals at a higher cost. Volume multiplies whatever you already have. Fix what you sell, to whom and why, and then volume starts to pay.

The second objection is "we'll hire a VP of Sales to fix it." A VP of Sales executes a strategy. If there isn't one, you have hired an expensive person to run faster in the same direction. The difference between the two roles is laid out in [fractional CRO vs VP of Sales](/fractional-cro-vs-vp-of-sales).

What you need is someone who can walk in, diagnose fast, and build the go-to-market infrastructure that moves the number. That is the job of a [fractional CRO](/services/fractional-cro), and it does not take a 6-month onboarding to start.

If revenue has been stuck and you can't figure out why, look past the sales team. Look at what they were asked to sell, to whom, and why it should matter to the buyer.

UVision knew all of that. It is the reason they never had a sales problem.
