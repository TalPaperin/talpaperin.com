Start as a billionaire.

I just read a rare interview with Ilan Ben-Dov. He is a man who once sat at the absolute peak of the Israeli economy, only to watch his entire empire wash away.

Who is Ilan Ben-Dov? He was not the "product guy" (that is Dov Moran, the inventor of the Disk-on-Key). Ben-Dov was the "structure guy." In the early 2000s, he controlled multiple companies with a total worth of $5.1 billion.

He built a massive empire through aggressive borrowing and a spiritual, Zen-like confidence. Then the market shifted. By 2014, the debt that built the tower became the weight that crushed it.

## The sandcastle fallacy

Ben-Dov compares his old empire to a sandcastle. He acquired Partner Communications for $1.8 billion, fueled almost entirely by debt.

Debt is a bet that tomorrow looks like today. While that holds, borrowed money makes you look like a genius: you control far more than you could ever pay for. When it stops holding, the same multiplier runs in reverse. The structure was never yours. You were renting it, and the landlord wants it back.

I'm not writing this to pick on a man who lost his fortune and is honest enough to talk about it. I'm writing it because I see founders building the same sandcastle right now, out of a different material.

In the AI age, it is tempting to borrow your growth from pure automation. Thousands of emails a day written by a model. A sequencing tool that never sleeps. Lead lists bought in bulk. For a while the dashboard looks wonderful: activity is up, cost per touch is close to zero, and nobody had to hire anyone.

But if you build on rented algorithms without human trust, you are building on sand. You don't own the platform, you don't own the inbox rules, and you don't own the attention of a buyer who has never spoken to a person at your company. One change on someone else's side and the pipeline you were counting on is gone by morning.

## How to tell if your pipeline is a sandcastle

Some signs, roughly in the order I run into them:

- Your meeting count went up and your win rate went down.
- Prospects show up to calls not knowing who you are or why they agreed to talk.
- No customer has ever referred another one to you.
- Your reps can't name a single buyer they could phone today and get an answer from.
- If your outbound tool were switched off tomorrow, you could not say where next quarter's revenue would come from.

Volume is easy to buy. Trust has to be earned by a person, one conversation at a time, and it is the only part of a pipeline that compounds.

## Human infrastructure

Ben-Dov admits his biggest mistake was failing to understand the social and political mood around him. He was so focused on the "deal" that he missed the shift in public sentiment.

Founders make a small version of this mistake every week. They stare at the deal, the term sheet, the CRM stage, and they miss the people. The champion who went quiet. The CFO who was never brought in. The customer who is unhappy and hasn't said so yet. None of that shows up in a dashboard. You only find it by being in the room.

This is why, the day before I wrote this, I was on-site in a lab with founders. Real business happens when you show up and listen.

So here is what I'd tell a CEO to do this week. Call five customers yourself, with no agenda beyond asking how it is going. Sit in on three sales calls without speaking. Visit one prospect in person if you can reach them. You will learn more than a month of reports would tell you, and the people on the other side will remember that you came.

## "So you're against automation?"

No. I'd be a strange person to say that, since I'm building an AI sales product myself.

As I build The Deal Mentor, I am steering clear of the sandcastles. We use AI to support the people, not replace them. It should do the preparation, the research and the grunt work, so that a human being walks into the conversation sharper. The conversation itself stays human. We focus on high-trust execution and stay away from hype built on borrowed foundations.

The same rule applies to how you build a sales function. Automate the repetitive work. Keep a real person, [properly hired and trained](/how-to-build-a-sales-team), in front of the buyer. And when the deal is large and the buying committee is long, as in [enterprise sales](/how-to-sell-to-enterprise), assume trust is the whole game. It is.

## What he kept

Ben-Dov traded the stress of a $5 billion empire for time with his children and his health. He realized the "tycoon" persona was not who he actually was.

He says: "At my core, I am still Ilan from Yad Eliyahu, selling copper wire."

I agree with him. A man selling copper wire has to look his customer in the eye, deliver what he promised and come back next month to sell again. Nothing about that has changed. No matter how much tech we add, the best business is still old school.
