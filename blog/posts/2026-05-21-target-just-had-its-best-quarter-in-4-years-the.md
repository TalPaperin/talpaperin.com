Target's sales just grew 5.6%. That's their best result in 4 years.

And this happened while:

- energy prices are rising
- consumers are under pressure
- competition from Walmart and Amazon keeps getting stronger

Retail sales in the US also climbed for 3 consecutive months.

So what changed?

Michael Fiddelke took over as CEO earlier this year.

And instead of trying to beat Walmart on price or Amazon on convenience, he pushed Target back toward what originally made people shop there in the first place: interesting products, discovery, emotion, experience.

In other words: positioning.

## Why this is the hard move

Look at what he didn't do. He didn't pick a fight on the ground where the competitor is strongest. Walmart's ground is price. Amazon's is convenience. A company that chases both ends up second on both.

Second place on someone else's strength is the most expensive position in any market. You pay to compete, and the customer still has no reason to pick you. The only way out is to stop playing their game and be first at something they are not.

That takes nerve, because positioning means saying no. No to the shopper who only wants the lowest price. No to matching every move a bigger rival makes. Most management teams can't do it. They would rather be a little of everything to everyone, which feels safe and reads to the buyer as nothing in particular.

## The B2B version

That's the part many companies completely miss.

I constantly see companies trying to solve growth problems by hiring more SDRs, adding more salespeople, buying more tools, building more dashboards and automating more outreach, before they even understand WHY buyers should care.

All of those are volume moves. They multiply whatever message you already have. If the message gives buyers a reason to choose you, more volume means more revenue. If it doesn't, more volume means more people hearing a pitch that doesn't land, at a higher monthly cost. You get the pattern I describe in [hired reps but no pipeline](/hired-reps-but-no-pipeline): a busy team with nothing to show for it.

In B2B, the Walmart and Amazon of your market are the cheap competitor and the incumbent with the big brand. If your pitch is "like the incumbent, but a bit better and a bit cheaper," you have chosen to fight both of them on their home ground. Your reps feel it on every call. They end up discounting, because price is the only difference left to talk about.

## How to tell positioning is your problem

Some checks you can run without hiring anyone:

- Ask five people in the company why customers buy from you. If you get five different answers, your buyers are getting five different pitches.
- Put your homepage headline next to two competitors' headlines and cover the logos. If you can't tell which is yours, neither can the market.
- Look at how deals end. Losing to "no decision" more often than to a named competitor means the buyer never saw a reason to act.
- Check discounting. If most deals close below list price, the team is using price to do the work positioning should have done.
- Look at the pipeline. Lots of early-stage deals that never advance is a pipeline inflated with polite interest.

If two or three of those are true, your salespeople are not the problem, and adding more of them will only make the problem more expensive.

## What to do about it

Go back to why customers bought in the first place. Fiddelke didn't invent a new Target. He pushed it back toward what originally made people shop there.

Your version of that is sitting in your customer base. Call your ten best customers and ask what they were trying to fix when they bought, what else they considered and why they chose you. Use their words. They are almost always different from the words on your website, and almost always better.

Then decide what you are not. Pick the fight you can win and name the buyers and the deals you will stop chasing. This is the step that hurts, and the one that makes the rest work.

Then rewrite the first two minutes of the sales conversation, the opening email and the homepage around that one reason. Train the team on it and listen to calls until the old pitch is gone.

Only then add volume. Getting that order right is what a real [go-to-market strategy](/services/go-to-market-strategy) is for.

## "Isn't positioning just marketing fluff?"

It would be, if it were a tagline exercise. I mean something much more practical: the answer to "why you, and not the other two?" that a rep can give in one sentence and a buyer can repeat to their boss. That sentence decides who you call, what you say, what you charge and which deals you walk away from. It is the most commercial decision a company makes.

Sometimes one experienced operator comes in, sees the actual problem in 10 minutes, changes positioning or strategy, and suddenly the entire company starts moving differently.

That's literally what I do. As a [Fractional CRO](/services/fractional-cro) I get brought into struggling companies when sales are down, positioning is weak, pipelines are inflated, growth stalls and management can't fully understand what's broken.

Very often the problem is not the salespeople.

The company simply forgot why customers buy in the first place.
