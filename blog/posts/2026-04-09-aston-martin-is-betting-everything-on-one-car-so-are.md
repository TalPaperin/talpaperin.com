Aston Martin is betting its future on one car.

The Valhalla.

$1M per unit. 999 units. ~$1B potential revenue.

That car is a financial recovery plan.

Now read that again. A company with years of financial instability is betting on ONE product to fix it.

Here's the part founders don't like to hear. This is exactly how many of you operate.

- "Once we launch this..."
- "This feature will change everything."
- "This version will finally bring in the revenue."

No. It won't.

## Revenue doesn't come from products

It comes from:

- real demand
- clear positioning
- buyers who actually need to change
- a sales motion that works

Do the Aston Martin math the way a sales leader would. $1M times 999 units is roughly $1B, and the word attached to it is "potential." Turning potential into money takes 999 separate buying decisions. Each one is a person with a million dollars to spend on a car, who decides to spend it now, on this car, from this company.

A great car is the easy part. Aston Martin needs people willing to spend $1M, right now, from them, instead of Ferrari.

That's the real game. Same as you.

Your spreadsheet has the same structure as theirs: price times number of customers equals the figure that saves the company. The price is in your hands. The number of customers is a count of decisions made by other people, and every one of them has an alternative, including keeping the money.

## Why founders keep making this bet

Building is what founders are good at, and it feels like progress. A feature has a spec, a deadline and a demo. Finding out why buyers don't move is slow and personal. It means hearing "no" and asking why.

There is a second reason. "The next release will fix it" cannot be disproved until the release ships. It buys a quarter of hope. Then the release ships, revenue stays where it was, and another feature takes its place on the roadmap as the one that will change everything. That loop can run for years.

The sales team learns to play along. When a deal stalls, the easiest explanation to hand a founder is a missing feature, because founders accept that explanation without argument. "They'll buy when we have X" rarely gets checked after X is delivered.

The signs that you are betting on the product to do the selling:

- Next year's revenue forecast depends on something that has not shipped yet.
- The lost-deal reasons in your CRM are mostly feature gaps, and nobody went back to those buyers once the feature was built.
- Your sales deck is a product tour.
- Demos go well and then nothing happens.
- Revenue is unstable: a good month when the founder personally closes something, then silence.
- You can explain in detail why the product is good, and you stumble on "why would they switch this quarter?"

## The question to answer instead

A better product is rarely what's missing. What's missing is an answer to one question:

Why would someone buy THIS from YOU now?

Not why it's good. Why they move.

Take the question apart, because each word is a separate piece of work.

**THIS.** What exactly is the buyer buying, in his own terms? He is paying for a result in his business. If your answer is a list of features, you have described what you built.

**YOU.** Why you and not the alternative he already knows? For Aston Martin the alternative has a name, and the name is Ferrari. Yours has a name too. Very often it is "what we do today."

**NOW.** What makes waiting expensive? If nothing does, he will wait. A deal with no reason for now is a deal for next year, every year.

## What to do this week

1. Take your last five wins. For each, write the customer's answer to why this, why you and why now. If you don't know, call and ask. Customers like being asked.
2. Take five deals that stalled after a good demo. Call the buyer and ask what would have to happen for this to become a priority. Do not pitch on that call.
3. Find the lost deals that were blamed on a feature you have since shipped. Contact them. Count how many come back. That count tells you what the missing-feature explanation is worth.
4. Remove the next big release from the forecast and look at what remains. That is the business your current sales motion produces.
5. Rewrite the first two minutes of your pitch so it opens with the buyer's reason to move.

Step 4 is the uncomfortable one, and it belongs in any honest [pipeline and forecast](/sales-pipeline-and-forecasting) review. The work behind steps 1 and 5, defining who buys and why they move, is the heart of a [go-to-market strategy](/services/go-to-market-strategy).

## "But our product really is better"

I believe you. I walk into companies all the time where the product is impressive, the team is strong, and revenue is still unstable.

A better product raises the ceiling on what a good sales motion can reach. It cannot do the motion's job. Before he signs, a buyer hardly experiences your product at all. He experiences a sales conversation, a proposal, a reference call, and a risk he will have to justify to his boss. When those are weak, the quality of the product never gets tested.

This is also why early traction misleads. A founder's passion closes the first deals, the team concludes that the product sells itself, and then the first hired salesperson cannot repeat it. I explain the mechanics in [why founder-led sales won't scale](/founder-led-sales-wont-scale).

The Valhalla may be a magnificent car. At $1M it had better be. It still has to be sold 999 times.
