Intel is buying Classiq. According to the reporting, 350 million dollars of that deal is not paid at signing. It pays out on performance, after the acquisition closes.

Read that again. A third of a billion dollars rides on targets you will chase as someone else's subsidiary.

That is an earnout, and it is becoming the default shape of a tech exit. The headline number sells the story. The structure underneath it says something quieter: the buyer is not fully sure the revenue will hold, so they are paying you to prove it does.

For a founder, this changes what the word exit means. The day you sign is not the day you are done. It is the day your sales engine takes on a new owner and a fresh quota, with your last big check attached to the result.

Here is where most companies get hurt. The revenue that got them acquired ran on the founder. The founder was the best closer, the relationship that saved the renewal, the one who knew which deals were real and which were theater. Inside a large acquirer, that founder is suddenly in planning meetings, not on calls. If the engine was really one person, the number slips in the first two quarters and the earnout evaporates.

The companies that collect the full price built something different, years before the banker called. A sales motion a stranger could run from documentation. Net revenue retention they can show on a chart, not describe from memory. A pipeline that does not depend on one person's calendar. That is the asset a buyer is willing to bet 350 million dollars on, because it keeps producing after the people who built it stop touching it every day.

If you are building toward an exit, this is the work that sets the terms. [A sales organization that runs without you](/services/fractional-cro) is not just what makes you acquirable. It is what lets you actually keep the money once you are acquired. The same muscle shows up in every clean exit: [a buyer pays for a revenue engine, not your technology](/blog/what-makes-a-company-acquirable).

The Intel number is a reminder that the terms have shifted. More of the price is contingent now. More of your payout depends on revenue you produce at the exact moment you have the least control you have ever had. Build the engine that keeps running when you are in the room next door. It is what gets you the offer. It is also the only thing that turns the headline price into real money.
