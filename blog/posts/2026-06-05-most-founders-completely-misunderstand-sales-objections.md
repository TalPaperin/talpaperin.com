A while back I was invited by Professor Philip Tran to teach Objection Handling to his students at the College of San Mateo in California.

The lecture eventually became an official part of the curriculum chapter on handling objections.

Objections are a huge problem in managing a deal, and most founders and salespeople completely misunderstand them.

A prospect says they are already working with a competitor, they don't have budget, or it's not a priority right now. The sales guy immediately starts hunting for the perfect comeback.

Wrong.

## The objection is the symptom

In most cases, the objection is not the problem. It's just the symptom. The real mistake was made weeks earlier.

It comes down to bad positioning, weak discovery, failure to expose pain, or failing to build real urgency and value.

Trace each of those three objections back.

**"We're already working with a competitor."** If this comes up late in the deal, nobody asked early what the buyer uses today and what they wish it did better. That is a discovery failure. And if the buyer sees you as interchangeable with what they already have, that is a [positioning](/services/go-to-market-strategy) failure: they were never given a reason to think of you as a different thing.

**"We don't have budget."** Companies find money for problems that hurt. "No budget" usually means the cost of the problem was never put into numbers, so there is nothing to weigh your price against. Or it means you are talking to someone who doesn't control money and never did.

**"It's not a priority right now."** The buyer is telling you that you didn't connect your product to anything they are being measured on this year. No urgency was built, because nobody asked what happens if they do nothing.

In each case the comeback, however clever, is being applied to a deal that was already lost. You are arguing with the outcome of a conversation that should have happened a month ago.

## Why battle cards and scripts don't fix it

That's why I've never liked the traditional playbook of building massive battle cards or memorizing generic responses.

Both approaches assume you know exactly what the prospect is going to say. You don't. Every prospect, deal and buying committee is completely different.

There's a second problem. A memorized rebuttal treats the objection as an attack. The buyer says "too expensive," the rep fires back the ROI line, and the buyer hears someone who isn't listening. The objection was an opening: the buyer telling you, maybe for the first time, what is going on in their head. A rep reading from a card closes that door.

And the words rarely mean what they say. "Too expensive" can mean "I don't see the value," "I can't get this past my CFO," "your competitor quoted less," or "I'm not the decision-maker and I'm embarrassed to say so." Four different problems. One card.

## How to find where your deals break

You can find your own pattern in a few hours.

1. List the objections from your last 20 lost or stalled deals. Don't sort them by type of objection. Sort them by the stage where they showed up.
2. For each one, ask: what would we have needed to know or establish earlier for this never to come up? Write the answer next to it.
3. Read down the second column. You will not find 20 different things there. You will find a short list: the wrong person in the room, no number on the pain, no reason to act now.

That short list is your real problem, and it has little to do with objection handling. It is the same list that makes a [sales forecast unreliable](/sales-forecast-always-wrong), because a deal with a hidden objection sits in the pipeline looking healthy until the day it dies.

## What to do instead

Move the work forward in the deal.

- **Qualify harder, earlier.** Before a demo, know who signs, what they use today, what the problem costs them and what happens if nothing changes. If a rep can't answer those four, the deal isn't ready for a demo.
- **Put a number on the pain, with the buyer.** Let them do the math out loud. A cost the buyer calculated is one they will defend to their own CFO.
- **Raise the predictable objections yourself.** If every deal runs into the incumbent vendor, bring it up in the first call: "You're probably using someone for this already. What made you take the meeting anyway?"
- **When an objection does come, ask before you answer.** "Too expensive compared to what?" tells you which of the four problems you have.

This matters even more in long deals with many stakeholders, where every extra person on the buying committee brings unspoken objections of their own. I cover that in the guide on [selling to enterprise](/how-to-sell-to-enterprise).

None of this means reps shouldn't prepare. They have to know the competitors, the pricing logic and the common concerns cold, and stay calm when a buyer pushes. I teach that. My argument is about where the effort goes. Most teams put nearly all of their training into the response and almost nothing into the discovery that would have prevented the objection. Flip that ratio and fewer objections show up, and the ones that do are real.
