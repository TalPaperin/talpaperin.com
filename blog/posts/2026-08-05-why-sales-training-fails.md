A CEO called me last month and asked if I do sales training.

I said no.

Then I told him I have personally trained more than 2,000 salespeople across four continents, and he got confused, which is fair. So let me explain the distinction, because it is the whole point, and it is costing you money right now if you do not see it.

## The two-day sugar high

Here is how sales training usually goes. You pay a trainer. They come in for a day or two. The room is energized. Your reps get a framework, a deck, a binder, maybe a role-play that everyone half-jokes their way through. On Friday your team feels sharper than they have in months.

By the following Wednesday, they are selling exactly the way they sold before.

Nobody is lying to you. The training was probably good. The problem is that a two-day event cannot change how a person behaves under pressure in a live deal three weeks later. Nothing was built to make it stick. The trainer got on a plane. Your reps went back to their inboxes. And the number did not move.

You did not buy behavior change. You bought a sugar high.

## Training is not the product

This is the part most people get backwards. The training itself is not the thing you actually want. What you want is a team that sells better, permanently. Training is one input. It is not the outcome.

Behavior only changes when someone senior watches it happen, coaches it in real deals, and holds the standard week after week until the new way of selling is just the way your team sells. That is not an event. That is a job. And it is a job the trainer was never hired to do, because they were already three clients down the road by the time your reps slipped back into their old habits.

## What 2,000 salespeople actually taught me

Over twenty-plus years I have trained SDRs, closers, channel partners and entire teams, in scrappy startups and in large organizations, in the US, the EU, the FSU and beyond. Thousands of people.

And the thing I learned is that the training was never what moved revenue. What moved revenue was everything that came after: sitting in on the calls, tearing apart the pipeline in the one-on-ones, catching the rep who quietly reverted to pitching instead of qualifying, and refusing to let the standard slide even when everyone was busy and the quarter was closing.

I measure myself on one thing. Not how good the session felt on the day. Whether the number was higher a quarter later. When you combine real training with real management, it is. Every time.

## Why I am not a trainer

A trainer sells you a session and leaves. I own the number.

When I train your team, I am not teaching theory I read in a book. I have carried a quota, built the playbook, and closed deals that look like yours. I teach exactly how I would run your deals, because I have run deals like them. Then I stay and manage the team until it works. That is the difference between a fractional CRO and a workshop, and it is the difference between a temporary bump and a permanent one.

If all you want is a box-ticking workshop, I am genuinely the wrong call, and you should keep your money. But if you want your team to sell better and keep selling better, you need the teaching and the managing from the same person, and you need them to stay until it holds.

That is the whole reason I stopped calling it training. Training is the easy half. Making it stick is the job.
