Today I visited our first client about a product I am launching in this post. I'm officially launching TheDealMentor.AI.

A virtual CRO. It replaces $500K in annual sales leadership with a software license: playbook, ICP profiles, competition analysis and battle cards, objection handlers, outreach sequences, call simulation and rep certification. All AI. All built from your specific company context.

And on day one I didn't post from an office. I went to a customer.

## Why I launched at a customer's desk

A launch post from the office gets you likes. A launch at a customer's desk tells you whether the thing works.

I have told founders for 20-plus years that their market does not live in their office, so I held myself to it. If a product claims to do the work of a sales leader, the test is a real company with real deals, and founders in the room who can say on the spot whether the output is right about their business.

MakhSuss. Two founders. Robotics implementation for factories and food facilities. 10 completed projects. They close deals. They're good at that.

That detail matters: this is a company that knows how to sell. What they needed was sharper infrastructure, in three specific places, and they are typical. Most founder-led companies I meet can close a deal and are missing the same things.

## What a company that already sells was missing

**Who exactly to target.** They had customers, but they hadn't defined their ICP (Ideal Customer Profile) clearly enough. The difference between "manufacturers" and "mid-size food processing facilities in the US replacing manual slaughter lines" is the difference between a 6-month sales cycle and a 6-week one. We built their ICP profiles in the intake.

Here is why the narrow version is faster. "Manufacturers" gives a salesperson nothing to act on. There is no list to build and no message to write, so every conversation starts with finding out whether there is a fit at all.

The narrow definition names a kind of company with a specific problem on a specific production line. The buyer recognizes his own situation, so the first meeting starts further along. And the proof you already have, your completed projects, speaks directly to him.

You can test your own ICP in one question. Could a new salesperson build a target list from it in an afternoon without asking you anything? If the answer is no, what you have is a description of a market.

**How to handle competition.** When a prospect brings up an alternative, you need a script, not an instinct. TheDealMentor.AI generated their battle cards: where competitors fall short, and the exact words to use when the comparison comes up.

Founders usually answer the competitor question by instinct, and they answer it well, because they know their market cold. The trouble starts with the first hire. The new salesperson gets the same question and improvises. Often he ends up offering a discount nobody asked for.

A battle card is the founder's instinct written down, so the second and third person selling give the answer the founder would have given.

**How to do outreach beyond their network.** Their pipeline was relationship-driven. Powerful, but limited. We built them cold outreach sequences for email and LinkedIn, written for their specific buyer and their specific proof.

A pipeline that comes from your network is the best kind until it runs out. Referred deals close quickly and at good prices. But you do not control the volume. You cannot decide to double your referrals next quarter.

Cold outreach is the part of the pipeline you can turn up on purpose. It works only when the first two pieces are in place: a specific buyer and specific proof. Generic cold email is the reason most founders believe cold outreach does not work.

## Total time: 20 minutes

Their reaction: "This is exactly what we needed. Why didn't this exist 3 years ago?"

A skeptical founder will ask how 20 minutes can produce anything serious, and it is a fair question. Twenty minutes produces the first written version, built from the company's own context. It does not produce the discipline of using it. Calling the list and sending the sequence are still work, and that work belongs to the team.

But the blank page is the most common reason founders never start. Getting rid of the blank page is the point.

The second objection is "we close deals fine, we don't need infrastructure." MakhSuss closes deals fine too. Infrastructure earns its keep on the day you want more deals than your network sends you, or on the day somebody other than a founder has to sell. That second day arrives for every company that grows, and I describe what goes wrong on it in [why founder-led sales won't scale](/founder-led-sales-wont-scale).

## Check where you stand this week

You do not need my product for this part.

1. Write your ICP in one sentence, as narrow as the food processing example: type of company, size, geography, and the specific problem.
2. List the alternatives you meet most often, including "do nothing." For each, write where it falls short and the exact sentence you say when it comes up.
3. Count what share of this year's deals came from people you already knew.
4. Write one cold email to one buyer who fits the sentence from step 1, using one piece of your own proof.

If step 1 turned into an hour of argument with your co-founder, good. That argument is the work, and it is the base layer of any [go-to-market strategy](/services/go-to-market-strategy). It also has to be settled before you hire your first salesperson, for reasons I go through in [how to build a sales team](/how-to-build-a-sales-team).
