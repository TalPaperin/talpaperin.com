There is an opinion piece going around CTech with a line most founders will flinch at: your next business might last six months, and that can be enough. The instinct is to argue. Sit with it instead, because the math it implies is the math a lot of companies should already be running and are not.

If a business might only last six months, every number tied to a far-off future stops mattering. Lifetime value over three years? Irrelevant if there is no year three. Payback in 18 months? That is a year past the finish line. The whole model flips from "how much will this customer be worth eventually" to "how fast does this customer pay me back." Most go-to-market plans are built for the first question. The market is increasingly asking the second.

## Payback is the number that survives a short runway

CAC payback is how many months of revenue it takes to earn back what you spent to acquire a customer. On a long runway you can tolerate a payback of a year or more, because time bails you out. On a short one, time is the thing you do not have.

When the horizon shrinks, a 12-month payback is a slow bleed and an 18-month payback is a death sentence. You are spending cash today for revenue that arrives after the company's own expiration date. The companies built to last six months, or to survive a year when they planned for five, are the ones whose customers pay back in weeks, not quarters.

So the question stops being "can we grow" and becomes "does each deal fund the next one fast enough to keep us alive." That is a sales design decision, and most founders never make it on purpose.

## What a payback-first motion looks like

Sell to the buyer who feels the pain now. Not the one who might need you next year. Urgency collapses your sales cycle, and a short cycle is the single biggest lever on payback.

Price for early cash, not just annual contracts. Upfront terms, setup fees, shorter paid pilots that convert. Revenue you collect in month one beats revenue you book for month ten.

Cut the cost of landing a customer, not just the price. A long, high-touch sales process with three demos and a six-week legal cycle destroys payback even when the deal is big. Shorten it ruthlessly.

Kill the deals that take forever. A deal that needs nine months to close is fine on a long runway and fatal on a short one. Qualify harder and walk away faster. I wrote about [how much pipeline you actually need to hit your number](/blog/how-much-pipeline-to-hit-number), and when runway is short, pipeline quality beats pipeline volume every time.

## This is not just for six-month companies

Here is the part that matters even if you plan to be around for a decade. A business that can pay itself back in weeks is a business that does not depend on the next round, the friendly market, or the patient investor. Short-payback discipline is what makes a long life possible. It is the same discipline, whether your horizon is six months or six years.

Building that motion is the core of what I do as a [fractional CRO](/services/fractional-cro): a [go-to-market engine](/services/go-to-market-strategy) tuned for fast payback, so the company funds itself instead of waiting to be rescued. Run your numbers on payback, not on a lifetime value you are assuming you will live to collect. The market just told you the horizon might be shorter than you planned.
