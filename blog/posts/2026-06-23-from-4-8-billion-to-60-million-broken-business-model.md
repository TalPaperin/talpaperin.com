From $4.8 billion to around $60 million. A 99 percent destruction of value.

If you are a CEO or founder, stop scrolling. There are some brutal lessons here.

Everyone will blame the technology. I think that is lazy analysis.

One of the biggest mistakes I see founders make is assuming that if they build something revolutionary, the business model will somehow take care of itself.

It will not.

## Hardware economics, SaaS pricing

Nano-X Imaging (NASDAQ: NNOX) was not selling software. It was selling expensive medical hardware. Yet it chose a SaaS business model.

Instead of selling the machines, they installed them first and planned to get paid over time, scan by scan. It looked brilliant on paper.

In reality, they had to manufacture the machines, finance the installations, wait for regulatory approvals, wait for hospitals to prepare infrastructure, wait for deployment, and only then start generating revenue.

Meanwhile the factory still needed to run. Employees still needed salaries. Investors still expected growth.

That is how you end up with incredible technology and terrible economics.

I understand the appeal, and so will any founder who has sat in front of investors. Recurring revenue is what everyone wants to see, and pay-per-use removes the buyer's biggest objection, the upfront price.

The catch is in what SaaS pricing quietly assumes. Software costs almost nothing to deliver to the next customer, and it can be switched on the day the contract is signed. The gap between spending money and earning it is short, so the vendor can afford to be paid slowly.

Hardware breaks both assumptions. Every unit costs real money to build before it earns anything. And between the factory and the first paid scan sits a chain of steps the vendor does not control: regulators, hospital infrastructure, deployment. Each one is a wait. During every wait, the company is the one carrying the cost.

Under a model like that, the seller carries the risk. The company pays for everything in advance and gets its money back last, in small pieces. That is a financing business, and a financing business needs a balance sheet built for it.

## Product-market fit is not the finish line

I have seen this happen more times than I would like. Founders spend years chasing product-market fit. Almost nobody spends enough time validating business-model-market fit.

- Can customers actually buy the way you have designed it?
- Can your company survive if implementation takes twice as long?
- Can you finance growth without constantly raising more money?

Here is how I would test each one.

**Can customers buy it this way?** Ask the people who sign, and the people in finance and procurement behind them. Buyers have budget categories, approval limits and purchasing habits that were there long before you arrived. A pricing model that doesn't fit an existing line in their budget needs a new approval path, and new approval paths take a long time. Founders design the model for the user who loves the product, and the user is rarely the one who has to get it paid for.

**Can you survive a doubled timeline?** Take your plan and double the time between signature and first revenue. Then check the bank balance at the end. If the company is dead in that version, the plan depends on everything going right, in an environment full of third parties who don't work for you. In enterprise and regulated markets, slower than planned is the normal case. The guide on [selling to enterprise](/how-to-sell-to-enterprise) explains why those cycles stretch.

**Can growth pay for itself?** Work out what it costs to land and deploy one customer, and how many months pass until that customer has paid it back. If every new customer makes the cash position worse for a long stretch, then selling faster brings the end closer.

## Signs your model is the problem

- Customers love the product in pilots and deals still stall at contract.
- Your sales cycle is long and getting longer, and the delays come from finance or legal, not from the user.
- Every growth plan you draw ends with "and then we raise."
- Your reps keep inventing custom payment terms to get deals signed.

Take the last one seriously. When salespeople improvise the commercial terms deal after deal, the market is telling you how it wants to buy.

## What to do about it

Before you scale anything, sell the model to ten real buyers the way you would sell the product. Show them the price, the payment structure and the contract, and watch where they flinch. That conversation costs nothing, and almost nobody has it until the factory is already built.

If you are heading into a new country or a new segment, do it again. A model that works in one market can fail in the next for reasons unrelated to the product, because buyers there pay differently. That check belongs at the start of any [market entry](/services/market-entry).

Then be willing to change the model while the product stays the same. Sell it outright. Take a deposit. Charge for installation. Lease through a third party whose business is financing. Pick the less elegant structure that gets cash in before cash goes out. Pricing and packaging are part of your [go-to-market strategy](/services/go-to-market-strategy), and they deserve the same rigor as the roadmap.

## "But recurring revenue is what investors pay for"

It is. And that pressure is exactly how good companies talk themselves into the wrong model.

Recurring revenue is valuable when the company lives long enough to collect it. A model chosen for how it looks in a valuation, without asking whether the cash holds out, is a bet that funding will always be available at the moment you need it. Sometimes it is. I wouldn't build a company on it.

The market does not reward brilliant technology. It rewards companies that survive long enough to monetize it.

Never forget this: a great product cannot save a broken business model.
