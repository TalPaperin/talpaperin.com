Nikesh Arora says 40 companies are racing to secure AI agents. At least 20 of them are Israeli. In the same few weeks, Reco raised 50 million dollars and Rein Security raised 25 million, both in the exact same category. If you are one of those founders, here is the uncomfortable truth. Your buyer cannot tell you apart.

They are looking at a dozen decks that say the same five things. Same problem statement, same architecture diagram, same logos, same "AI-native" claim, same demo flow. The category is a knife fight, and the deck does not win a knife fight. The sales motion does.

So stop trying to win on the slide that everyone else also has.

## Buyers do not choose the best product. They choose the clearest one.

In a crowded category the buyer is not comparing features. They stopped reading features three vendors ago. They are drowning, and they will pick whoever makes the decision easiest to defend internally. That is a sales problem, not a product problem, and it is the one most technical founders refuse to work on because they are convinced the product should speak for itself.

It does not. Not when 39 other products are saying the same words.

The vendors who win a crowded market are rarely the ones with the best technology. They are the ones who show up with the sharpest point of view, name the buyer's specific pain better than the buyer can, and remove risk from the decision. The product has to be good enough. The sale is what closes.

## Three places to actually differentiate

Pick a wedge, not a market. "Security for AI agents" is what 40 companies say. "The control layer for finance teams whose AI agents touch payment systems" is a sentence only you say. A narrow wedge you dominate beats a broad category you are twentieth in. You expand later, from strength.

Sell the buyer's problem back to them in their words. The vendor who describes the prospect's situation most precisely wins the room, every time. Most founders describe their own product instead. Flip it. Spend the first half of every call proving you understand their world better than the competitor who called yesterday.

Take the risk off the table. In a field of 40 unknowns, every buyer is terrified of picking wrong. Whoever makes "yes" the safe choice wins. A tight proof of value with a clear success metric beats a longer feature list. I wrote about why [deals stall at the proposal stage](/blog/why-deals-stall-at-proposal), and in a crowded category the stall is almost always fear of a wrong bet, not price.

## The motion is the moat

When 20 companies have similar technology and similar funding, the durable advantage is not the product. It is the go-to-market machine: how precisely you target, how clearly you position, how fast you move a deal from first call to signed, how reliably you prove value before the buyer has to commit. That machine is buildable, and most of your 39 competitors are not building it. They are polishing the demo.

This is the work I do as a [fractional CRO](/services/fractional-cro), and it is exactly what a [sharp go-to-market strategy](/services/go-to-market-strategy) is for: finding the wedge, sharpening the message, and building the motion that makes a buyer choose you out of a crowd of lookalikes.

Forty companies entered this category. Most will not die because their product was worse. They will die because their selling was average in a market that punishes average harder than anything else.
