A good name in the kehilla wins you every customer who already knows you. It does nothing for the buyer in another state who has never heard of you. Business development is the work of reaching that second buyer on purpose, and it is where most frum businesses leave the most money on the table.

I am a frum, shomer Shabbos Yid from Eretz Yisrael. I have spent over twenty years building and running revenue for B2B companies on five continents, and a real part of that work has been with frum and heimishe business owners in the US.

## What business development means here

People use the phrase for everything from handing out cards at a dinner to hoping a cousin makes an introduction. I mean something narrower: choosing the partners, channels and markets worth pursuing, building those relationships deliberately, and turning them into signed, repeatable revenue.

Most frum businesses never had to do this. The owner's reputation brought in the work, the kehilla bought from its own, and referrals filled the calendar. That carries a business surprisingly far. Then the referrals level off, because there are only so many people who already know you, and nothing sits underneath to replace them.

Networking will not fix that. Neither will luck. You need a way to reach people who owe you nothing.

## Reaching the market outside the kehilla

A frum business that owns its corner of the kehilla often freezes the moment it has to sell to the secular, mainstream or international market. The buyers are different and so are the rules. A name that opens every door at home means nothing to a procurement manager who has never heard it.

I have spent twenty years selling into rooms where nobody knew me yet. An established heimishe business has rarely had to build that muscle, and business development depends on it. I take frum-owned companies into the US mainstream, the EU and beyond, without asking the owner to stop being who he is. The trust-first way you already sell stays. What gets added is a way to start conversations with strangers and carry them to a signature.

The traffic runs the other way too. Outside companies ask me how to sell into our world without stepping on it, and I wrote that up in [how to sell to the frum market](/blog/selling-to-the-frum-market).

## By community

The pattern repeats, but every community has its own version of it. Here is how business development looks in each of the ones I work with.

### Lakewood

Lakewood, home to Beth Medrash Govoha, is one of the largest and fastest-growing Torah communities in the world, and its frum-owned businesses run from real estate and e-commerce to wholesale, finance and B2B services. Plenty of them are strong inside the frum world and stall the moment they need customers who never heard of them. The jump is across Ocean County into the wider market, out to national buyers, or into the US mainstream from a Lakewood base.

### Boro Park

Boro Park is one of the densest and most established frum business hubs anywhere, with a commercial heart along 13th Avenue and family names trusted for two or three generations. Many of those businesses dominate inside the kehilla and freeze when they try to reach the wider New York market or national buyers. Taking an established, relationship-built business into the mainstream without losing what makes it yours is specific work, and I do it.

### Williamsburg

Williamsburg is heavily chassidish, Yiddish is a first language for many, and the business world is tight and largely self-contained, spanning manufacturing, wholesale and real estate. A lot of owners dominate inside the kehilla and hesitate at the wider New York market. I do not speak fluent Yiddish and will not pretend to. I have spent years inside the heimishe business world, and I take Yiddish-first businesses to mainstream partners and national buyers.

### Monsey

Monsey is really a whole region: Spring Valley, New Square, Kaser, Suffern, Airmont. It is packed with frum businesses that are great at what they make and unsure how to sell it past the neighborhood. Business development here usually means expanding across Rockland into the wider market, going national, or selling into the US mainstream from a Monsey base, to buyers who never davened with you.

### Crown Heights

Few communities are as globally connected as Crown Heights, and few leave as much of it on the table. It is the worldwide center of Chabad-Lubavitch, with a shliach network that reaches every corner of the globe, so its businesses start with connections on six continents. My work is turning that network and your own reach into new markets, partners and distributors.

### Flatbush

Flatbush is a large, mixed community, yeshivish and professional, stretching across Midwood and Ocean Parkway, with a growing set of B2B and online companies next to the retail, medical and real estate base. Plenty of these businesses are strong locally and unsure how to reach past the community. The targets are usually the wider New York market, national accounts or the mainstream. Getting a Flatbush business beyond the neighborhood is the jump I help owners make.

### Five Towns

Cedarhurst, Lawrence, Woodmere, Hewlett and Inwood form an affluent community thick with finance, professionals, real estate and B2B services. Many Five Towns businesses are strong inside a tight, well-off network and want to reach past it, to national accounts, new markets or the US mainstream. The connections and the reputation are already there. I turn them into a business-development engine.

### Kiryas Joel

KJ, in Monroe in Orange County, is a Satmar chassidish village, Yiddish-speaking and among the fastest-growing communities in the country, with manufacturing, wholesale, retail and real estate businesses connected out to Monroe and Woodbury. Many are strong inside the village and want the wider Orange County market or national buyers. Taking a heimishe, Yiddish-first business into the mainstream without losing what makes it yours is specific work.

### Passaic

Passaic and neighboring Clifton are a growing yeshivish community within commuting distance of New York, with professionals, services, retail, real estate and a rising number of B2B and online companies. Plenty of them are solid locally and want to reach beyond the community and the New York commuter belt. The step into the wider market or national accounts is the one I help Passaic owners take.

### Baltimore

Baltimore is one of the older, more established frum communities in America, anchored by Ner Yisroel and centered around Park Heights and Pikesville. Its businesses tend to be steady and well-run, in a relationship-driven market where the cost of doing business sits well below New York. Many are strong locally and want regional or national markets, or the US mainstream. That takes more than warm referrals.

### Los Angeles

Frum Los Angeles is spread across Pico-Robertson, Hancock Park, La Brea and the Valley, a mix of chassidish, yeshivish and modern-orthodox with a strong entrepreneurial streak. Many of its businesses are strong within the community and carry national or international ambitions. The usual targets are the wider Los Angeles market, the West Coast or national accounts, and local connections alone will not get you there.

### Miami

Miami's frum community is one of the fastest-growing in the country, swelled by an influx from New York and the northeast. A business scene full of new arrivals is a strong moment to build business development properly, into the wider Florida market, national accounts and beyond. A hot market hides a lot. The businesses that turn this momentum into a working engine will not depend on the market staying hot.

### Chicago

Chicago's frum community is centered in West Rogers Park and Peterson Park and reaches out into Skokie, an established Midwestern kehilla of professionals, services, healthcare, retail and real estate. Many of these businesses are strong locally and ready to reach past the community, into the wider Midwest, national accounts or the mainstream. What is usually missing is a way to win business that does not start with a warm referral.

## Questions frum business owners ask me

### Will you contact me or my partners on Shabbos or Yom Tov?

No. I am shomer Shabbos. There are no calls, meetings or messages from me on Shabbos or Yom Tov, ever. I build the work around the Jewish calendar without being asked, because it is my calendar too. Nobody will book a partner meeting for Friday afternoon on your behalf.

### Everyone in my kehilla knows everyone. How discreet is this?

In a close kehilla, your business is your business, and I treat that as part of the job. Which partners you are talking to, where your revenue is leaking and what you plan to do next stay between us.

### Can a heimishe business really win partners outside the community?

Yes. Taking frum-owned companies from selling inside the kehilla to selling into the US mainstream, the EU and international markets is one of the things I do best. The relationships that built you do not carry over to a buyer who has never heard your name, so we build a way to earn that buyer from zero.

### How does working together go?

Hands-on and mostly remote, built around your time zone and the Jewish calendar. I own the work the way a senior VP would, without the six-figure hire. It runs on a monthly retainer with no long contract, and the tiers are public on the [pricing page](/pricing). It starts with a straight conversation: [tell me where revenue stalled](/contact) and I will tell you the emes about what I see.

## The kehilla built you. It should not be your ceiling.

Nothing here asks you to leave the community that made your business what it is. Keep every relationship. Keep the way you give your word and stand behind it. A market made only of people who already know you has an edge, though, and most owners reach it sooner than they expected. The ones who keep growing decided who they wanted to sell to next, and then built the road to get there.
