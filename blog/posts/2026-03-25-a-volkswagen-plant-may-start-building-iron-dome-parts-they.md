A car factory in Osnabruck, Germany, originally built to produce the T-Roc Cabriolet, is reportedly in advanced talks with Israel's Rafael to manufacture Iron Dome launcher trucks, missile components and power generators.

2,300 jobs saved. A production line repurposed. A defense supply chain born in a place no one expected.

But here's what this story is really about: Volkswagen didn't stumble into this. They saw the signals, and acted.

## The signals were public

Israel's Iron Dome has proven itself on the world stage, intercept after intercept, in real combat, in real time. Nations across Europe and beyond are watching. They want this technology. The demand is already there, and it's growing fast.

None of that was secret. Anyone could see it. What Volkswagen did differently was ask a question: "How do we get in front of this wave?"

I call that strategic business development. Manufacturing is only how the answer gets delivered.

Look at the logic from their side. They had a plant, a trained workforce and a production line, and the future of all of it was in doubt. Jobs don't need saving otherwise. So they looked at where demand is growing and asked what those assets could do there. A line that builds cars can build launcher trucks.

That is the whole move. Start from the demand and work back to what you already have.

Most companies do it the other way around. They start from the product they have and go looking for someone who might want it.

## Why most companies wait for the crisis

Most companies wait for crisis to force the pivot. The smart ones see the shift coming and move first.

Waiting is comfortable, and there are reasons for it. The current business still pays most of the bills. Existing customers still call. Next quarter's forecast looks acceptable. Every sign that the market is moving can be explained away as one lost deal, one slow month, one customer who "wasn't a fit." Changing direction means admitting that the plan needs changing, and nobody's bonus depends on saying so.

There is also a mechanical reason inside the sales organization: the signals arrive in pieces. One rep hears a new objection. Another loses to a competitor nobody had heard of. An account manager notices that renewals are getting harder. Each person holds one piece, and it is nobody's job to put the pieces together. So the company learns about the shift from the revenue number, which is the last place the news arrives.

Then the crisis forces the move, and you pivot with little cash and no negotiating position. Move early and you get what this plant got: advanced talks with a serious partner while there is still something to offer.

## How to tell whether you are reading the signals

Ask yourself:

- Do you know why you lost your last five deals, in the buyer's words and not the rep's?
- Has the kind of customer who buys fastest from you changed in the past year?
- Is there a segment that keeps showing up inbound and gets treated as a distraction because it is not in the plan?
- Are the same new questions and objections coming up across different sales calls?
- When did your leadership team last spend a meeting on where demand is growing, as opposed to how to close this quarter?

If you cannot answer the first two, all you are reading is the dashboard. A dashboard reports what already happened.

## What to do about it, starting this week

1. **Collect the signals in one place.** Ask every rep and account manager for the things they heard from buyers this month that surprised them. Put it all on one page and read it yourself.
2. **List your real assets.** I mean what sits underneath the product: your team's expertise, your customer relationships, your technology, your ability to deliver. Volkswagen's asset was a plant and people who know how to build vehicles. The convertible was only what the plant was first built for.
3. **Match assets to growing demand.** Where are buyers already spending money, and what could your assets do there? Write down two or three options.
4. **Test the best option cheaply.** Hold ten conversations with buyers in that segment before you change a line of code or a line of the price list.
5. **If it holds, rebuild around it.** Decide who you target, what you say and who does the selling. Then build the sales infrastructure to execute fast.

That sequence is what I have spent 20-plus years doing with companies across industries: identify where the real revenue opportunity lives, restructure the [go-to-market strategy](/services/go-to-market-strategy) around it, and build the team and the process that can carry it. If nobody in your company owns that job today, it is one of the clearer signs covered in [when you need a fractional CRO](/when-do-you-need-a-fractional-cro).

## "We can't chase every trend"

Correct, and you shouldn't. A company that pivots every quarter is in as much trouble as one that never moves.

But look at how narrow the Volkswagen move is. A vehicle plant is in talks to build trucks, components and generators. That sits close to what it already knows how to do. And the demand behind it was proven in real use before the talks began.

Those are the two tests for any signal you pick up. First, is the demand already there, with buyers who are spending? Second, can you serve it with capabilities you already own? Two yeses make it a move worth planning. A yes on the first alone means it is somebody else's opportunity, and you should let it go without regret.

Whether you are a founder selling without a sales team, a startup trying to [crack enterprise](/how-to-sell-to-enterprise), or an established company that needs a revenue reset, the question is the one Volkswagen asked. How do we get in front of this wave?

Ask it while the choice is still yours.
