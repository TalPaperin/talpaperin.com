$6.6 billion in debt. Chapter 11 filed. Shares down 70% in a single day.

The cruel irony is that QVC didn't have a bad product. They had a bad sales motion, and they held onto it for too long.

They pioneered the format. They owned the category. They even bought their biggest competitor, HSN, to consolidate the market.

And while they were busy protecting what they built, their customers quietly walked over to TikTok Live, Whatnot and Instagram Shopping.

The channel changed. QVC didn't.

## Live shopping didn't die

Notice what did not happen. People did not stop buying from a live host who shows them a product. The idea QVC invented is alive and well on TikTok Live, Whatnot and Instagram Shopping. Demand for it is fine. What expired was the way QVC reached the buyer.

Buying HSN is the most instructive move in the story. When a category leader buys its biggest rival, it is defending itself against the competitor it knows. The threat came from platforms that were never on the competitor list, because they did not look like competitors. Consolidate a category that buyers are leaving and you own a bigger share of an emptier room.

The B2B version of this happens all the time. A company fights its two known rivals for years, feature for feature, and then loses to a different way of buying.

## Your sales motion has an expiration date

Here's the hard truth most founders and CEOs don't want to hear: your sales motion has an expiration date.

The way you generated revenue last year (the channels, the messaging, the motion) is not guaranteed to work next year. Or even next quarter.

Markets shift. Buyers change how they buy. New competitors don't play by the old rules.

There are a few plain mechanisms behind it:

- A channel that works attracts everyone, and then it stops working. When every vendor sends the same cold sequence to the same job title, response drops for all of them.
- Buyers learn. The message that sounded fresh two years ago is now what all your competitors say.
- The decision moves. A purchase that a department head used to approve now passes through procurement or finance, and a pitch built for the department head has nothing in it for them.

None of these arrives as an event. Each one shows up as a slow decline in a number that somebody has a local explanation for.

The better a motion worked, the harder it is to let go of. The team was hired to run it and the commission plan rewards it. The people who built it are senior now, so questioning the motion means questioning them.

## Early signs that the date is getting close

- The same activity produces less. It takes more calls or more emails to get one meeting than it did a year ago.
- You hold your own against the old competitors and lose deals to "we went another way," with a name you don't recognize or no name at all.
- Your best source of deals is still the founder's network, or a channel set up years ago, and nothing newer has taken its place.
- Your customers' buying process has gained steps and approvers, and your sales stages have not changed.

One of these on its own is noise. Several together are a trend.

The companies that survive are the ones with the most adaptive revenue engine, and those are not always the ones with the best product. Being adaptive starts with three questions.

- Who's watching how your buyers' behavior is changing?
- Who's challenging your go-to-market before it becomes a liability?
- Who's asking the uncomfortable questions before the numbers force the conversation?

In most companies the honest answer is "nobody." The head of sales is paid to hit this quarter with the motion that exists. Marketing runs its calendar. The CEO is busy elsewhere. If you cannot put a name next to each of those questions, the work is not being done. ## How to challenge your motion this week

1. **Write the motion on one page.** Who you target, through which channels, with what message, and the steps from first contact to signature. Most companies have never written it down, which is part of why it never gets questioned.
2. **Pull the conversion numbers for each step over the last four quarters.** Look at the direction. A number that is acceptable and falling deserves more attention than one that is poor and stable.
3. **Talk to buyers.** Ask ten recent customers how they found you, who else they considered and how they decided. Ask the same of five who did not buy.
4. **List how your buyers can now solve the problem without a vendor like you.** Those are your TikToks.
5. **Test one new channel or motion on a small scale.** Give it a deadline and a target number, and run it while the old motion still pays the bills.

Steps 1 and 4 are the opening moves of a proper [go-to-market strategy](/services/go-to-market-strategy) review.

## "But what we do still works"

It probably does. QVC's motion worked well enough to make them the owner of their category, and it kept working until it didn't.

The time to test a replacement is while the current motion can fund the test. Once the numbers force the conversation, you are experimenting with no money and a board that has run out of patience.

I am not telling you to throw away what works. Keep it running, and test the next thing beside it.
