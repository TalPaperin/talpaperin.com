Most cold email is bad for one reason. It is about the sender. Here is who we are, here is what we built, here are seventeen features, please book thirty minutes. Nobody cares. Your prospect deleted it before your first comma.

Relevance beats volume every time. I would rather send 30 emails a buyer cannot ignore than 3,000 a spam filter eats. So stop trying to sound impressive and start sounding like you actually understand their world.

## The framework: one problem, one proof, one ask

Every cold email I write has four short parts, and it fits on a phone screen without scrolling.

- **Relevance line.** One sentence that proves you know who they are. Their role, their market, a specific trigger. Not "I saw you are the VP of Sales." More like "You just opened a US office and your reps are still working Israel hours."
- **The problem.** Name the pain they already feel. Lead with their problem, never your product. If they nod, you have earned the next line.
- **One proof.** A single, concrete result. "I booked 22 qualified meetings for a Series A security company in a quarter." One number beats a paragraph of adjectives.
- **One ask.** A single, low-friction question. "Worth a 15-minute call next week?" Not three options, not a calendar link dump, not "let me know your thoughts."

That is it. No em dashes, no jargon, no "just circling back." One problem, one proof, one ask.

## Before and after

Before: "Hi Dana, my name is Tal and I run a sales consultancy that helps B2B companies accelerate revenue through proven go-to-market methodologies. We offer a range of services. Do you have 30 minutes this week to learn more about what we do?"

After: "Hi Dana, you are hiring two SDRs in the US while your pipeline is still mostly Israeli logos. That gap is where most of my clients were stuck before we fixed targeting. I booked 22 qualified US meetings for a Series A team last quarter. Worth 15 minutes next week to see if the same play fits you?"

Same length. The second one gets replies because it is about her, not me.
