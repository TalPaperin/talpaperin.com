Outside companies do one of two things with the frum market. They ignore it, or they insult it by accident. Both are expensive, because this is one of the most loyal and fastest-growing markets there is, and it remembers how you treated it.

I am a frum, shomer Shabbos Yid from Eretz Yisrael, and I have built and run sales for B2B companies on five continents for over twenty years. A real part of that work has been inside the frum and heimishe business world. So when a company asks me how to win frum customers, I answer as someone who sells for a living and as one of the customers.

The rules are different from the open market. Getting them wrong costs you more than one deal.

## Trust comes before the product

In the open market you can win a stranger with a better price and a sharp pitch. Here a good name comes first. Deals run on trust and reputation, built over time, often through a shared connection. Cold pressure does not work. A respected reference does.

That changes where you start. Before the campaign and before the price list, find the people whose word carries weight with the buyers you want, and earn their confidence honestly. One introduction from the right balebos opens doors that no amount of outreach will.

## Respect the values, or pay for it

An ad that is loud or not tznius does worse than fail. It offends, and word travels fast in a connected kehilla.

Outsiders usually make this mistake with good intentions. They take the creative that worked everywhere else and run it unchanged. Before anything goes out, have someone from inside the community look at it. That includes the images, the wording and the salesperson you plan to send.

## Build around the calendar

Shabbos, Yom Tov and the zmanim shape when and how people buy. They are not negotiable, and a sales process that ignores them is dead on arrival.

Do not set a meeting for Friday afternoon. Do not plan a launch for the weeks when Yom Tov empties the office. A frum buyer notices immediately whether a vendor had to be told these things, and a vendor who gets them right unprompted has already shown how he will treat the account.

## The community talks

The kehilla is the network. One happy balebos brings ten more. One chillul of trust closes the whole market to you.

For a new entrant, that cuts both ways. You cannot bury a bad experience, and you do not need a huge budget to spread a good one. Deliver what you promised to your first customers and they will do part of your selling for you.

Discretion belongs here too. In a world where everyone knows everyone, a vendor who talks about one customer's business to another loses both.

## It is many markets, and some of them speak Yiddish

Treating "the frum market" as one audience is the next mistake. Williamsburg and Kiryas Joel are heavily Satmar chassidish, with Yiddish as a first language. The Five Towns is affluent and largely modern-orthodox. Crown Heights is the worldwide center of Chabad-Lubavitch, with a network that reaches every corner of the globe. Lakewood, home to Beth Medrash Govoha, is one of the largest Torah communities in the world. One pitch will not land the same way in all of them.

Then there is the language. A large part of the heimishe business world runs in Yiddish, down to the handshake. I do not speak fluent Yiddish and I will never pretend that I do. Plenty of people speak Yiddish and cannot sell. Understanding how this world buys matters more, and where the language matters in the actual selling, you put the right person in the room.

## A market with a long memory

Selling into the kehilla or out of it, the frum side of the deal is the part outsiders get wrong and insiders take for granted. A market this loyal rewards the companies that earn their place in it. It has a long memory for the ones that did not.
