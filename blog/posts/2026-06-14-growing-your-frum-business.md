Most frum businesses stall at the size one person can personally carry. The founder built it on hustle and a good name, every deal still runs through him, and the week has no more hours to give. Working harder stopped helping a while ago.

You built something real. Now you want to expand it without all of it resting on your shoulders. I am a frum, shomer Shabbos Yid from Eretz Yisrael who has built and run revenue for B2B companies on five continents, and getting frum and heimishe owners past that point is a real part of my work.

## Why the business stopped growing

The story is nearly always the same, whatever the town. A business takes off because the founder is good at what he does and people trust him. Referrals come from shul, from the neighborhood, from a name that travels. For years, growth feels automatic.

Then it flattens. Nothing went wrong. The business reached the edge of what one person can sell and oversee. The founder is the whole sales department, growth is all referrals, and no system exists to bring in a customer who has not already heard of you. Often the entire operation lives in the founder's head.

A frum owner carries one more thing. A large mishpacha and communal life are part of who you are, and they do not leave much time to build a sales machine on the side. Extra hours are not available. They would not solve it anyway.

## What growing past the founder takes

It takes a machine: a sales system, a team that runs it, and a leader who owns the number so you do not have to be in every deal. That is how a good local business becomes one that sells everywhere.

- Expand into the wider market: the US mainstream, the EU and beyond
- Put the team, the process and the numbers in place to grow with control

## Two ways to expand

**Deeper into your own market.** Word of mouth reaches the people who are near the people who already know you. A sales function reaches the rest of the frum market.

**Out to the wider market.** This is the harder one. A frum business that owns its corner of the kehilla often freezes when it has to sell to the secular, mainstream or international market. I take frum-owned companies into the US mainstream, the EU and beyond. I build the sales motion, hire the team and open the markets, without asking the owner to stop being who he is.

The order matters. A business that cannot sell without its founder at home will not manage it in a market where nobody knows the founder. So the machine comes first, and the map comes second.

## By community

The ceiling is the same everywhere. What sits under it changes from one community to the next.

### Lakewood

Lakewood runs on real estate and development, and around that sits a deep bench of Amazon and e-commerce sellers, wholesalers, home-care services and finance shops. A lot of them started as a side parnassa and grew faster than anyone planned. Then they flattened, because every deal still runs off the founder's phone. With Toms River, Jackson, Howell and Brick growing alongside, the room is there for the owners who build a sales machine.

### Boro Park

Boro Park businesses are often family-run and generations deep: importers and wholesalers, retail up and down 13th Avenue, accounting and insurance shops, printers. They grow to a point on family, reputation and a loyal local base, then plateau because the founder is still the whole sales engine. Scaling past that, especially when you also want to reach outside the kehilla, needs a system and someone senior to own it.

### Williamsburg

Williamsburg is one of the most concentrated chassidish business centers in the world, built on real estate, property management, construction, retail, wholesale and a deep web of B2B services. Businesses here grow fast on family, community and reputation, then hit a wall where the founder is still the entire sales and decision engine. The closeness of the kehilla is an edge at home. It becomes the wall when growth means dealing with the outside market on its terms.

### Monsey

The typical Monsey growth story is a business that took off on the founder's hustle and relationships, then flattened because every deal still runs through one person. It could be a service firm, a store on Route 59 or an e-commerce company, and many are run entirely out of the founder's head. Add the pull of a large mishpacha and communal life, and little time is left to build the sales machine.

### Crown Heights

Crown Heights is the center of the Lubavitch world, and its businesses have built-in connections on six continents. Many grow to a point on those relationships and that reach, then plateau because no engine sits underneath the network. Scaling means building the process that captures all those connections and works them, so growth stops depending on the founder personally knowing everyone.

### Flatbush

Flatbush's commercial strips run along Avenue J, Avenue M and Coney Island Avenue, and its businesses serve Flatbush, Midwood, Marine Park and beyond. Many are run well, and run entirely on the owner. The common pattern is a solid business built on the founder and the neighborhood that flattens once it outgrows word of mouth. Breaking through takes a sales function that does not need the owner to know everyone personally.

### Five Towns

The Five Towns is one of the more affluent and business-heavy frum areas anywhere, with a commercial heart along Central Avenue and a reach over to Far Rockaway. The talent and the money are here. A common story is still a business that grew nicely on the founder's network and then plateaued, because it never built a sales function beyond him and nobody senior owns revenue.

### Kiryas Joel

Kiryas Joel is young and among the fastest-growing communities in the country, with real entrepreneurial energy in manufacturing, retail, services, real estate and wholesale. Businesses there often grow fast on community and hustle, then reach the point where the founder is still the entire operation. Scaling past that while also reaching outside the village needs a system and someone senior to run it.

### Passaic

Passaic businesses are scaling, and many are hitting the same wall: growth built entirely on the founder and on referrals, with no system underneath. The common pattern is a good business, carried by the founder and the community, that flattens once it outgrows word of mouth. I build the process, bring in and train the right people, and own the number until it runs.

### Baltimore

Baltimore is a stable, relationship-driven market with a cost of doing business well below New York, and its frum businesses tend to be steady and well-run. A common story is a solid, relationship-built business that plateaus because it never built a sales engine beyond the founder. Scaling past that ceiling, without the owner being in every deal, is the work I own.

### Los Angeles

Frum Los Angeles sits inside a huge, car-dependent market, with the community spread from Pico-Robertson and Hancock Park to La Brea and the Valley. The spread and the mix are an opportunity. The common pattern, though, is a business that grew on the founder's network across those pockets and plateaued, because it never built a sales function beyond him.

### Miami

Frum Miami is booming, from Miami Beach and Surfside to North Miami Beach, Aventura and Bal Harbour, helped by Florida's tax and lifestyle pull. Its real estate, finance, hospitality, kosher food and retail businesses are growing quickly on relationships and the influx. They will plateau the same way others do, when the founder is still every deal. Build the sales function while the growth still comes easily.

### Chicago

Frum Chicago is centered in West Rogers Park, around Devon and California, and reaches out into Skokie. It is a stable, relationship-driven market. A common story there is a solid, well-established business that plateaus because it never built a sales engine beyond the founder. The missing piece is usually a senior sales leader to own the motion and a system to run it.

## Questions frum business owners ask me

### Does growing mean compromising on Shabbos and Yom Tov?

No. I am shomer Shabbos, and there are no calls, meetings or messages from me on Shabbos or Yom Tov, ever. The work is built around the Jewish calendar from the start. A growth plan that only works if you answer the phone on a Friday night is not a plan I would write.

### Will people in the kehilla know I brought someone in?

Not from me. In a close kehilla your business is your business. Where you are stuck and what you are fixing stay private.

### Do I have to leave the heimishe market behind to grow?

No. The point is to open the wider market without losing the kehilla that built you. The customers who trust you stay your base. What changes is that new business no longer depends on them alone. If you want to understand how outsiders should approach our market, I covered that in [how to sell to the frum market](/blog/selling-to-the-frum-market).

### I hired a salesperson once and it failed. Why would this be different?

It is one of the most common places a heimishe business gets stuck: a salesperson was hired, it did not work, and nobody knew how to manage him or fix it. The hire is rarely the whole problem. There was no process to hand him and no one senior to manage him to a number. I hire, train and manage the salespeople, and I fix or replace what is not working.

## Parnassa should not cost you everything else

For a frum owner, parnassa is how you take care of your family and support what matters. A business that can only grow by taking more of you works against the reason you built it. Build the machine, put someone senior in charge of the number, and the business can keep growing while you go back to the parts of it, and of your life, that only you can do.
