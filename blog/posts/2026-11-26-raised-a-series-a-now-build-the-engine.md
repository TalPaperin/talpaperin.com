This week the Israeli cyber startup Mave closed a $28 million Series A. The press release is the fun part. It is also the last easy thing that happens for a while.

A Series A is not a prize. It is a contract with a clock on it. Someone just handed you cash against a promise: that you can turn it into repeatable revenue fast enough to raise again, higher, in roughly eighteen to twenty-four months. The round does not prove you can do that. It bets that you can.

Watch where that money usually goes. More engineers. A bigger product roadmap. A pile of new SDRs hired in a month because the board wants to "see the funnel grow." All of it feels like progress. Most of it is motion, not a motion.

Here is what the next raise will actually be priced on.

Can you show that revenue does not depend on the founder being in every deal? Can a rep who is not you take a lead from first call to signature using a playbook that exists on paper? Is there a number, and does one senior person own it, or does everyone own it, which means no one does? That is the engine. Before Series A you were allowed to sell on charisma and founder hustle. After Series A, founder-led selling is a ceiling, and your investors can see it even when you cannot.

The trap is subtle. Founder-led sales works well enough early that it hides the problem. You close deals because you are the most convincing person in the building and you know the product cold. Then you hire two reps, they miss, and you decide they were bad hires. Usually they were not. You handed them a product and a territory, not a repeatable way to sell. There was nothing to hand them because it only ever lived in your head.

So what does the first year post-raise look like if you do it right? You spend some of that $28 million, or whatever your number is, building the thing that outlives you in the sales seat. The ideal customer defined tightly enough to say no. One motion that works, written down, not improvised per deal. The first real sales hires, trained on that motion, managed by someone who has built a team before. A pipeline math that connects activity to the number so you know in month three whether you will hit plan, not month eleven.

That is unglamorous next to shipping features. It is also the only version of the next eighteen months that ends in a Series B instead of a bridge round.

If you just raised and you are deciding where the money goes, the sequence matters more than the spend. Get [the go-to-market right first](/services/go-to-market-strategy), then hire into it, in the order I lay out in [how to build and scale a sales team](/how-to-build-a-sales-team). And if runway or the clock is tighter than the deck suggested, the math changes fast, so read [how sales math shifts when time is short](/blog/sales-math-when-runway-is-short) before you staff up.

The $28 million is in the account. The question your next investor will ask is whether you built an engine with it, or just ran faster on the one that was always going to stall.
