Before you start selling, you must understand the exact sales chain you are selling in, your location in that chain, the exact value you bring to the next link, and the value that link brings to the one after it.

Here's what I mean:

- If you grow bell peppers and sell them directly to consumers, that's B2C.
- If you sell those peppers to a retailer, that's B2B.
- If you sell them to a distributor that sells them to the retailer, that's B2B2C.
- If you sell them to an importer that sells them to the distributor that sells them to the retailer, that's B2B2B2C (unless the end retailer is an online one, then it's B2B2I2C).
- If you sell your peppers to the government that exports them for you and sells directly to the retailer, that's B2G2B2C.

I have a few more of those, but you see the point. Same pepper every time. Five different sales jobs.

## Each chain is a different sale

Each sales chain is different and requires a completely different approach.

Selling a pepper to a consumer is about the pepper: how it looks and what it costs. Selling the same pepper to a retailer is about shelf space, margin, and whether you deliver on time every week. The retailer does not eat your pepper. He resells it.

Add a distributor and the conversation changes again. Now your buyer cares about how fast the product moves through his warehouse and how many retailers will take it without a fight. Add an importer and you are discussing paperwork, payment terms and who carries the risk while the goods are in transit.

The product never changed. The buyer, the questions he asks, the length of the sale and the person who signs all did.

This is where founders get into trouble. They build one pitch, usually the one aimed at the end user, and take it to every link in the chain. The distributor listens politely to ten minutes on product quality, then asks the only question he cares about: what do I make on this?

## What every chain has in common

HOWEVER, they all have something in common.

VALUE.

What is it that you bring to your immediate customer, and what is HIS value? How can he profit down the line? In other words, you will have to help your customer sell your products.

Most of the work hides in that last sentence. Your deal with the next link does not end when he signs. It ends when his customer buys, and that customer's customer after him. If the product sits in his warehouse there is no second order, and the first one becomes an expensive lesson for both of you.

So the pitch to the next link has two parts. Part one is what he gets from you: margin, reliable supply, support when something goes wrong. Part two is what he will be able to say to HIS buyer. If you cannot write that second part for him, do not expect him to invent it. He carries other suppliers' products too, and he will push the one that is easiest to sell.

## How to tell you have not mapped your chain

Signs I see in companies that skipped this step:

- You describe your customer as "the market" or "the end user," but the invoice goes to someone else entirely.
- Your sales deck talks about product features, and your actual buyer is a reseller who cares about margin and turnover.
- You signed a distributor and then nothing happened. No orders, no calls returned.
- Your salespeople cannot say who their buyer sells to, or what that next buyer needs to hear.

Any one of these means you are selling to a link without understanding the links that come after it.

## Map it before you sell

This takes an afternoon. You can do it this week.

1. Draw the chain from your warehouse (or your server) to the final user. Every company that takes ownership of the product, or has to approve it, goes on the page.
2. Mark where you sit and who pays you. That is your customer, whatever your marketing says.
3. For every link after you, write one line: how does this link make money on my product?
4. For your immediate customer, write the value you bring him AND the pitch he will use on the next link.
5. Find the weak point. If one link has no good reason to move your product, the chain breaks there, and that is where your time should go.

Then build the sales material to match. One version for your direct buyer, plus the tools he needs to sell onward: pricing that leaves him a margin, answers to the objections he will hear, proof he can show.

This map is the first page of any real [go-to-market strategy](/services/go-to-market-strategy). It matters even more when you [enter a new market](/services/market-entry), because the chain in a new country is rarely the one you know from home. An importer appears, or a government body, and the pitch that worked last year stops working.

## "My product is good enough to sell itself"

I hear this objection a lot. The product is so good that the distributor, the retailer and the end customer will all see it immediately.

Maybe the end customer will. The links in the middle judge your product by what it earns them. A great pepper that leaves the retailer no margin stays off the shelf.

The same thing happens in [enterprise sales](/how-to-sell-to-enterprise), where the chain runs inside one company: the person who uses the product, the manager who asks for budget, the people who approve the purchase. Each one has to pass your product to the next with a reason attached, and you are usually not in the room when it happens.

Know your chain. Know your place in it. Then make sure every link after you has a reason to sell.
