What's every salesman's dream? The best-case scenario?

A client with a clear need, a burning, immediate and costly problem, with the money, the authority and the desire to solve it, who comes to HIM. But is that enough?

Here is that dream, playing out in one room. Several large multinational companies, Philips and Strauss among them, took part in a seminar in Israel. During it they presented quite a few problems they are facing right now, mostly in Industry 4.0 and advanced manufacturing, though not only there.

Think about what that means for anyone who sells. The buyers stood up and described their own problems, out loud, in public. Nobody had to cold call them.

And those problems can be solved fairly easily with IoT and SaaS, plus a few tweaks to the actual process.

So the deals should close themselves. They don't.

## Why the dream scenario still falls apart

Being in sales means being able to match a solution to a problem. To do that you have to analyze the problem first, and the way you analyze it is discovery.

A willing partner on the other side helps a lot. It is not enough.

Here is what happens when a buyer hands a salesperson a problem on a plate. The salesperson hears the first sentence, recognizes something his product does, and starts pitching. From that second on he has stopped learning. He is answering the problem as it was presented on a slide, which is the short, public, cleaned-up version.

The real problem sits behind it. Which production line. What it costs them each month. Who inside the company already tried to fix it, and why that failed. Who owns the budget and who can block it. None of that is on the slide, and none of it comes out unless someone asks.

A big company that presents a problem at a seminar will hear from many vendors afterwards. Most of them will send a deck about themselves. The one who shows up with good questions gets the second meeting.

## It is all about THEM

Remember that it is all about THEM, and not you and your product.

Every salesperson will tell you he knows this. Then check your own team's last five first meetings and count the minutes. How long did your side talk, and how long did the buyer? In most companies I walk into, the salesperson talks for most of the call and reports a great meeting because the buyer nodded.

A buyer with a burning problem does not need a tour of your features. He needs to feel that you understand his situation better than the other vendors he spoke to. That comes from two abilities:

- Asking the right questions.
- Listening carefully to the answers.

The second is harder than the first. Plenty of salespeople ask a decent question and then spend the answer preparing their next line. The buyer notices.

## A conversation with a structure and a purpose

The third ability is the skill and expertise to TALK. To have a conversation, a well-structured and purposeful one.

Structure does not mean a script read off a screen. It means you walk in knowing what you must learn before the meeting ends. From a first discovery conversation, I want a salesperson to come back with answers to these:

1. What exactly is the problem, in the buyer's own words?
2. What does it cost them today, in money or in time?
3. What have they already tried, and why didn't it work?
4. Who else is involved in the decision, and what does each of them care about?
5. What happens if they do nothing for another year?
6. What would have to be true for them to move now?

Six questions. If a rep comes back from a meeting without the answers, the deal in the CRM is a hope with a company name attached.

The purpose part is just as plain. Every conversation ends with a specific next step that both sides agreed to, with a date on it. "They loved it, I'll follow up" is how a dream lead dies quietly.

## How to check your own team this week

You can run the first pass yourself. Take three recent deals that came in warm (a referral, an inbound request, a buyer who described his problem at an event) and did not close. For each one, ask the rep the six questions above.

If the answers are thin, your problem is the conversation, and more leads will not fix it. You are wasting the ones you already get. This is one of the most common things I find when a company tells me it has [hired reps but has no pipeline](/hired-reps-but-no-pipeline): the opportunities were there, and the discovery was missing.

Then fix it where it lives, in the calls. Listen to recordings. Sit in on meetings. Getting that habit into the team is a big part of [building a sales team](/how-to-build-a-sales-team) that can sell without the founder in the room, and it is daily work for a [fractional CRO](/services/fractional-cro).

## "But the buyer already told us what he needs"

That is the objection I get from founders, and I understand it. The buyer wrote a brief. He presented the problem at a seminar. Why question him again?

Because what he told you is what he tells everyone. Your competitors heard the same presentation. If your proposal answers only the public version of the problem, it will look like every other proposal on his desk, and the decision goes to price.

Discovery is how you earn the right to propose something specific to him.

So yes, the dream client exists. A clear need, a costly problem, money, authority, desire, and he walks in on his own.

He is still not enough. Add the right questions, careful listening, and a structured, purposeful conversation.

THOSE should be enough.
