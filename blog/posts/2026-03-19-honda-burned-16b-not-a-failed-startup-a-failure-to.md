Honda burned $16B.

Not a failed startup. Not a bad product. Honda.

They canceled multiple EV models they had already invested heavily in, because reality didn't match the plan:

- EV demand in key markets slowed.
- Government incentives changed.
- China moved faster and cheaper.
- Margins didn't work the way the models assumed.

So instead of pushing forward and hoping it fixes itself, they took the hit. Wrote down billions. Cut plans. Reset direction. Even the CEO is taking a pay cut.

Now here's the part most people miss.

## Honda did not fail at building cars

They failed at reading the market correctly at the time they acted.

Timing. Demand. Economics. Buyer behavior. Read the list of reasons again and you will see that every item is about the market. None is about engineering. The assumptions underneath the plan were wrong: how many people would buy, when, at what price, and against which competitor.

This is exactly where most B2B companies get it wrong, and the product is rarely where the mistake sits. The mistake sits inside one long question: will people actually buy this now, from us, instead of doing nothing or choosing someone else?

## What it looks like inside a B2B company

I walk into companies with a strong product, a real need in the market and a good team. Deals still don't close. When I dig in, I find some version of these:

- The urgency isn't real. The buyer agrees the problem exists, and he has lived with it for years.
- The alternative, doing nothing, is stronger than switching.
- The competition is misunderstood. The team prepares for the vendor it meets in demos, while the buyer is weighing it against an internal project or simply against keeping his budget.
- The pricing doesn't match perceived value.

So pipeline builds. Revenue doesn't.

And leadership starts thinking that something is wrong with sales. Sometimes it is. Often the go-to-market assumptions are off, and the sales team is executing a plan that was wrong before anyone made the first call.

It stays hidden for a long time, and here is why. Pipeline measures interest, and interest is cheap. A demo and a few kind words do not test the four assumptions above. They get tested only at the moment someone has to sign and pay, which comes late.

Honda's version: the models were designed and the money was invested, and then the market answered. Your version: the reps are hired and the quota is set, and then the quarter answers.

The signs that sales is taking the blame for a market problem:

- Deals reach the proposal stage and go quiet. You are not losing them to a named competitor.
- The most common loss reason in your CRM is "no decision" or "timing."
- You already replaced a sales leader or a few reps, and the numbers look the same.
- Every rep has stuck deals, and they are stuck at the same stage.

The last one is the clearest signal I know. When one rep fails, look at the rep. When they all fail in the same place, look at the plan.

## Pressure-test it before the market does it for you

This is what I check when I am brought in. You can start on it this week.

1. **Demand.** Take your last ten closed deals, won and lost. For each one, write down what forced the buyer to act at that moment. If you cannot find a trigger even in the wins, your urgency is assumed.
2. **Positioning.** Ask five customers what they would have done had they not bought from you. Their answer is your real competition, and it is often "nothing."
3. **Deal reality.** Go through the open pipeline deal by deal. Is there a named person with budget? A reason to change? A date that belongs to the buyer and not to your quarter? Take out everything that fails.
4. **Economics.** Compare the price you need with the value the buyer believes he is getting. If every deal needs a discount to move, the market is telling you something.
5. **Whether revenue is actually there or just looks like it.** Rebuild the forecast using only the deals that survived step 3. That is your real number.

The mechanics of step 3 and step 5 are in my guide to [sales pipeline and forecasting](/sales-pipeline-and-forecasting). If the first two steps come back empty, the fix is upstream of sales, in the [go-to-market strategy](/services/go-to-market-strategy) itself.

## "We just need better salespeople"

Sometimes that is true, which is why the work starts with a diagnosis.

But think about what Honda would have gained from better car salesmen. Nothing. The demand, the incentives and the margins were what they were. Put stronger closers on top of a wrong go-to-market assumption and you get the same stuck pipeline with a higher payroll. A company can go through two rounds of hiring and firing before anyone questions the plan the reps were handed. It is the pattern behind most cases of [hired reps but no pipeline](/hired-reps-but-no-pipeline).

## Take the hit early

What I respect in the Honda story is the decision. They saw that reality did not match the plan, and they stopped. They wrote the money down, cut the plans and reset direction, and the CEO took a pay cut along with it.

Most leadership teams do the opposite. The money is already spent, the board has seen the slide, so they push forward and hope it fixes itself.

Honda can absorb a $16B correction. Most companies can't. A company with limited runway that spends a year executing a wrong assumption may not get to write a second plan.

That's why I get brought in earlier, or sometimes too late. To pressure-test demand, positioning and deal reality, and to find out whether revenue is actually there or just looks like it.

If your pipeline exists but revenue doesn't follow, that's the problem.
