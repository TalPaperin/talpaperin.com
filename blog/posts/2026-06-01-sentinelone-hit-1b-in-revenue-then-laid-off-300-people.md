And they're laying off 300 people.

Not a startup running out of money.

Not a company that can't find customers.

Not a company whose product doesn't work.

A billion-dollar company. Growing. Publicly traded.

Still cutting 10% of its workforce.

This is where many founders get confused.

They think growth solves everything.

It doesn't.

## Growing what, exactly?

I've seen companies with no sales and too many employees.

I've seen companies with growing sales and too many employees.

I've seen companies raise millions and still collapse.

Because the problem is rarely revenue alone. The problem is efficiency.

A company can grow and still become less profitable.

A company can add customers and still destroy shareholder value.

A company can hire hundreds of people and discover later that half of them were never needed.

That's why "we're growing" is one of the most dangerous sentences in business.

Growing what? Revenue? Profit? Headcount? Costs? Market share?

They're not the same thing. And the trouble is that inside a company they all go up together for a while, so nobody separates them.

## How a growing company gets inefficient

I don't know the inside story at SentinelOne and I won't pretend to. What I know is the public picture: past $1 billion ARR, forecasting another 20% growth, and still laying off hundreds of employees. That combination alone should end the idea that growth protects you.

What I can describe is how it happens in the companies I do see from the inside.

**Growth hides waste.** When revenue is climbing, nobody asks whether the newest SDR team is paying for itself, because the total looks good and the board is happy. Every department's request gets approved on the strength of the top line.

**Headcount becomes the strategy.** Pipeline is short, so hire more reps. Reps aren't closing, so hire more managers and an enablement team. Each hire answers a symptom. Nobody goes back to check whether the last ten hires fixed the last symptom.

**Hiring runs ahead of revenue.** You staff for the plan. If the plan says double and you deliver half of that, you have built a company for a business that doesn't exist, and a growth rate that sounds healthy is suddenly a crisis.

**Costs are sticky and revenue isn't.** A salary is owed every month. A deal can slip a quarter.

Put those together and you get a company that is bigger every year and worse every year, with a growth number that keeps everyone calm until the day it doesn't.

## Check your own numbers

You don't need a finance team for this. Take an hour and look at five things.

- **Revenue per employee, this year against last year.** If you grew and this number fell, you bought your growth with headcount.
- **Sales and marketing cost against the new revenue it produced.** Spend rising faster than new revenue means each dollar of growth costs more than the last one.
- **Output per rep.** How many of your salespeople are at quota? If a few carry the number and the rest are along for the ride, more reps won't help. Run the real cost of a seat through the [sales cost calculator](/sales-cost-calculator) and compare it with what your median rep brings in.
- **Payback.** How long does a new customer take to pay back what you spent winning them? If that is getting longer, growth is eating cash faster each quarter.
- **Cost growth against revenue growth.** Put the two lines on one chart. If costs are the steeper line, you already know how the story ends.

Then ask the question every company runs into at some point:

Are we building a business? Or are we just building a bigger version of our problems?

## What to do before the market does it for you

Layoffs are what efficiency looks like when it arrives late. The earlier version is quieter and much less painful.

Stop treating a hire as the default answer. Before approving a new sales role, ask what the existing team would produce if the bottom half performed like the middle. That gap is usually bigger than what the new hire would bring, and closing it takes training and management attention, which cost far less than another salary. If you have reps and no results, the fix is rarely more reps. I go through the real causes in [hired reps but no pipeline](/hired-reps-but-no-pipeline).

Tie every new cost to a number it is supposed to move, and a date. Check it on that date. Most companies approve spending with a business case and never open the business case again.

Report efficiency to yourself as loudly as you report growth. Put revenue per employee and cost per new customer on the same slide as ARR. What gets shown to the board gets managed.

## "We're a growth-stage company. Efficiency comes later."

That's the standard reply, and investors have encouraged it for years. Spend ahead, win the market, clean up afterward.

Sometimes spending ahead of revenue is right. If you know that a dollar in produces more than a dollar back, and you know how long it takes, pour money in. That is investment. Spending when you don't know those numbers is hope with a budget.

And "later" has a way of arriving all at once. A company at $1 billion ARR, forecasting 20% growth, is still cutting 10% of its people. If it caught up with them, it will catch up with you.

I've spent 20 years being brought into companies that were growing, hiring, raising money and celebrating.

Many of them didn't need more salespeople.

Many didn't need more marketing.

Many didn't need more developers.

They needed someone willing to look at the numbers and ask uncomfortable questions. That is a large part of what a [Fractional CRO](/services/fractional-cro) is for: owning the revenue number honestly, including the parts of it nobody wants to discuss.

Usually nobody wanted to hear the answers.
