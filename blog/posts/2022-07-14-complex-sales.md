Many people talk about sales. Unfortunately, most of the talk is about easy, straightforward B2B or B2C.

How about something more complex than your typical cold calling, email sequences, SDRs and AEs?

Something like this.

I was hired by a DIY manufacturer to expand their global sales. They were doing great in the US and the EU, and it was time for new markets. It took me about a month to come up with the strategy, and I advised them to enter the Eastern European market.

Romania and Moldova were chosen as good starting points.

That's the WHERE.

## The WHERE comes before the HOW

Notice the month. It went into strategy before a single sales conversation took place.

Founders hate this part. They want activity: calls made, emails sent, a salesperson on a plane. But the choice of market is the biggest sales decision you will make, and every later step inherits it. Pick the wrong country and a very good team will spend a year proving it.

A month of work on WHERE means answering unglamorous questions. Where is there demand for what we make? Who sells it there today, and through which channels? Do buyers purchase directly from a manufacturer, or only through local distributors? Which country is small enough to learn in and connected enough to lead to the next one?

The answer for this manufacturer was two countries. A region was the goal, and two countries were the start. That is what a beachhead means: a small piece of ground you can hold, from which the larger move begins. Announcing "we are entering Eastern Europe" and spreading a thin budget across a dozen countries is how a company ends up with a dozen half-opened markets.

## Now the HOW

Using the power of LinkedIn, I eventually got in touch with the Ambassador and the Economic Attaché in Romania. They helped me set up a road show and many meetings with relevant decision makers, both buyers and local distributors. They also took part themselves and helped with their knowledge and their connections.

The same happened with the Moldovan Ambassador, who came to our HQ in Israel and made the proper introductions.

As a result, those two countries became a beachhead for an expansion into the Eastern European market.

It worked where cold outreach would not have, and the reason is simple. A buyer or a distributor in a market you have never sold in has no reason to trust you. He does not know your brand, and he cannot check you with anyone he knows.

An introduction from an ambassador or an economic attaché changes the meeting before it starts. You arrive as a company that an official has agreed to stand next to. The people in the room came because someone they take seriously asked them to. And the person who made the introduction knows the local market: who the serious distributors are, who decides, how business gets done there.

That is the mechanism. You borrow credibility and local knowledge you could not build yourself in under a few years.

It matters because a complex sale almost never has one buyer. There are buyers, there are distributors who will sell onward, and sometimes a government body sits in the middle. Someone has to open those doors in the right order. A sequence of five emails will not do it.

## Cold calling is great. It has a ceiling.

Cold calling is great. Spamming people also works. Facebook and LinkedIn ads, SEO... that's nice.

But that is NOT how you do sales on a larger scale. B2B2B, B2B2G, B2G: they all have a different set of rules.

I am not against outbound. For a simple sale, with one buyer and a short cycle, a good SDR team is the right machine. The mistake is assuming the same machine opens a country, signs a distribution network or wins a government customer. Those sales have more people in them and a longer timeline, and the first meeting is usually earned through a relationship.

Signs that you are running a simple-sale playbook on a complex sale:

- Your product reaches the end customer through a distributor or a government body, and your outreach is aimed at end users.
- Your reps book meetings in a new country, and the meetings go nowhere because the person on the other side cannot buy from a foreign supplier directly.
- Your choice of market was made by whoever answered an email first.

## What to do this week

1. Stop adding countries. Pick one or two that you can win and that lead somewhere.
2. Write down the chain: who buys from you, who they sell to, who has to approve.
3. List the institutions whose job is to help companies like yours in that market, starting with embassies and economic attachés. Contact a named person with a specific request.
4. Ask for introductions and a structured visit: a road show with meetings set in advance. A trip to "see who we meet" is tourism.
5. Go in person, with the people who made the introductions beside you if they are willing.

This is the core of [market entry](/services/market-entry) work, and it begins with a [go-to-market strategy](/services/go-to-market-strategy). The lead list comes later.

## "We don't have time for a month of strategy"

I hear this from founders who then spend a year in the wrong market.

A month of thinking is the cheapest part of an international expansion. Flights, a local hire, a contract signed with the wrong distributor: those are the expensive parts, and they all come after the decision. Get the WHERE wrong and you pay for it in every one of them.

Simple sales has its playbook, and it is a good one. Complex sales runs on a different set of rules. Find out which game you are in before you start playing.
