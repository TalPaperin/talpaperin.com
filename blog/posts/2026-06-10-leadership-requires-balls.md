![Tal Paperin pointing at medicine balls on a rack at the gym](/blog/img/leadership-balls.jpg)

These are balls. Big ones.

I have those. So does my wife. That's part of why I married her.

Having them matters. Especially if you lead anything, business, sales, politics.

My clients work with me partly because I have them. It's a big reason they pick me over a safe full-time US hire.

The great ones had them too. Alexander the Great. Tamerlane. Ben Gurion. MacGyver. Shaft. Optimus Prime. The list goes on.

It used to be the baseline. VP Sales, SDR lead, President, Prime Minister, you could not do the job without them.

Then something happened. Easy times. Atrophy.

Now "leadership" is something they try to teach you in a workshop. At a nice retreat in Bali.

Nope. That is not how it works.

Training, hardship, overcoming difficulty and yourself, that is what builds a real leader.

## What it looks like in a revenue org

Let me be specific, because "courage" on a slide means nothing.

In sales leadership it comes down to a handful of moments, and they all have the same shape: there is a comfortable option and a correct one, and they are different.

- Telling the board the forecast is wrong before the quarter ends, when there is still time to act and it is still embarrassing to say.
- Firing the rep everyone likes who has missed quota for a year.
- Telling your biggest customer no when they ask for something that will sink your margin.
- Telling the founder that his favorite market, feature or hire is the thing holding revenue back.

None of these needs special knowledge. Every manager knows the right call. What stops them is the five minutes of the conversation itself, and a surprising number of people in senior roles will do almost anything to avoid those five minutes.

## What atrophy looks like

It rarely looks like cowardice. It looks like reasonableness.

"Let's give him one more quarter." "Let's not upset the customer right before renewal." "The board doesn't need that level of detail." "Let's see how the pilot goes before we decide."

Each of those can be the right answer once. As a habit, they add up to a company where nothing bad is ever said out loud, so nothing bad is ever fixed. The underperformer stays. The forecast stays optimistic. The discount becomes the price. When the truth finally arrives it comes from outside, from the bank balance or the investors, and by then it is a rescue job. A [sales forecast that is always wrong](/sales-forecast-always-wrong) is very often a courage problem dressed up as a data problem: people knew, and nobody said it.

Easy times produce this. When revenue grows without much effort, a leader can go years without a single hard call, and get promoted for it. Then conditions turn, and the company discovers that the person in the chair has the title and none of the scar tissue.

## Why the safe hire is often the risky one

A full-time executive has a lot to protect. A salary, a title, equity that vests over years, a relationship with the CEO he has to live with every day. All of that pulls toward the comfortable option. Telling the founder he is wrong is expensive for someone whose mortgage depends on the founder's mood.

Those are the incentives, and most people in that seat soften.

I sit in a different seat. My engagements run month to month, I have other clients, and I am paid to own the revenue number. If I tell you what you want to hear, I fail at the only thing I was brought in for. So I say the uncomfortable thing in week one, and then I do the uncomfortable work, in the room, with your team. That is part of the difference between a [fractional CRO and a full-time VP of Sales](/fractional-cro-vs-vp-of-sales), and for a lot of my clients it is the part that matters most.

## So what if you don't have it?

What if all you have ever known is a cushy life, a cushy job, a cushy environment?

Then you acknowledge it. And you outsource it.

Or you wait for the hard times to restart the cycle.

Acknowledging it is not weakness. Plenty of excellent founders are builders: brilliant at product, good with investors, liked by their teams, and unable to fire anyone or look a customer in the eye and hold a price. Knowing that about yourself is worth more than any workshop, because it tells you which seat to fill with someone built differently.

A quick self-test. Think of the hard decision in your company that everyone already knows about. There is always one. How long has it been waiting? If the answer is measured in quarters, you have your diagnosis.

You can also start rebuilding the muscle, the only way it gets built: by using it. Pick the smallest overdue hard conversation and have it this week. It doesn't get pleasant. It does get faster.

## "Isn't this just an excuse to be a jerk?"

No. Loud isn't brave. The manager who shouts at his team in the pipeline review and then caves to every customer demand has it exactly backwards.

What I'm describing is mostly quiet. It is saying a true thing plainly, to the person it concerns, early enough to matter, and then staying in the room to deal with what follows. The people on the receiving end usually thank you later, because they knew too.

I work with the ones who acknowledge it, or who waited too long and now, in tears, ask me to rescue their company and their revenue. If you are not sure which group you are in, the guide on [when you need a fractional CRO](/when-do-you-need-a-fractional-cro) lists the signs.

The cycle restarts either way. Hard times come, and they make leaders out of whoever is left. I'd rather you didn't wait for them.
