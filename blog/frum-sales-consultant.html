A Frum Sales Consultant and Fractional Sales Leader

By Tal Paperin· June 14, 2026· 9 min read
FrumSalesHeimishe BusinessBusiness Consultant

If sales still run through you and a pile of referrals, that is a ceiling, not a strategy. Most frum business owners I meet already feel it. The name is still good and the phone still rings, but revenue has stopped climbing, because there are only so many hours in the owner's week.

I am a frum, shomer Shabbos Yid from Eretz Yisrael. I have built and run sales for B2B companies on five continents for over twenty years, and a real part of that work is with frum and heimishe business owners. I come in as a sales consultant who does the work, usually as a fractional sales leader.

The good name was doing the selling

Charm and luck do not add up to sales. A sales function is a repeatable system run by trained people who are managed to a number. Most frum businesses never built one, and the reason is flattering: the owner's good name was doing the selling, so nobody needed to.

It shows up the same way almost everywhere. Everything runs through the balebos, and there is no system when he steps away. Word of mouth is wonderful until you need customers who have never met you. Somebody hired a salesperson once, it did not work, and nobody knew how to manage him or fix it.

None of that means the business is weak. It means the selling machine was never built.

What I do

I build it, run it, and hand you a sales function that does not depend on you being in every deal.

  • Build a sales process and playbook to replace the guesswork
  • Hire, train and manage salespeople who close
  • Set up the CRM, the pipeline and an honest forecast
  • Own the number with you, hands-on, from first call to signed deal
  • Fix or replace what is not working, without the drama

Two neighbors of this page: finding partners, distributors and markets outside the kehilla is in business development for frum businesses, and getting the whole company past what one founder can carry is in how to grow and expand your frum business.

What a fractional sales leader is, and when you need one

A fractional sales leader is a senior revenue boss you bring in part time instead of paying a full VP of Sales a fortune. I own the strategy, the team, the pipeline, the forecast and the number, hands-on, from diagnosis to closing. I do the work. I do not hand you a deck and leave.

For a frum business owner that means VP-level sales leadership without a six-figure hire. The model is laid out on the fractional CRO page.

You need one when any of these is true:

  • You are tired of carrying sales alone, and the business cannot grow past your own calendar
  • You hired a VP or a salesperson and the number did not move
  • The business has the product and the ambition, and nobody senior owns the revenue function
  • You want a real sales team and do not want to bet the business on a risky VP hire

If none of those sounds like you, you probably do not need me yet.

The frum part is not a gimmick

You have probably hired help that did not get you. A consultant who set a meeting for Friday afternoon. One who did not understand why Yom Tov empties your office for two weeks, or who treated parnassa like a video game score.

I will not pick up on Shabbos and I will not push a meeting on Yom Tov. I build the work around the Jewish calendar without being asked, because it is my calendar too. A handshake with a fellow Yid means something to me.

Sharing a value system does not close deals, though. Skill does. The work is the same hard revenue work I do for every client. With me you also get a leader who understands the heimishe world you sell in, which I describe on the frum business consultant page.

By community

How a business sells depends a lot on where it grew up. This is what I see, community by community.

Lakewood

In Lakewood a huge amount of business still moves on referrals: from shul, from the neighborhood, from a good name that travels fast in a town this size. The pattern I keep seeing is a founder who can sell anything himself and nobody else who can carry a deal. Go after the wider market or national buyers and the warm introductions run out, with no process waiting to replace them.

Boro Park

Business in Boro Park runs on relationships and a name trusted along 13th Avenue and across the kehilla for years, sometimes for two or three generations. That works until the buyer is mainstream, out of town, and does not run on the same handshake. The work is building the motion that reaches and wins those buyers without losing what makes you the obvious choice at home.

This is exactly what I fix, hands-on. Monthly, no contract, no exit fines. If revenue is stuck, tell me where.

Let's Talk

Williamsburg

In Williamsburg, selling is personal, in Yiddish, and built on a name that carries weight in the kehilla. It works beautifully until the buyer is a mainstream, English-speaking, out-of-town stranger. I do not speak fluent Yiddish and I will never pretend to. I work with Yiddish-speaking owners in English, and where the language matters in the actual selling, we put the right person in the room. What I add is twenty years of selling into rooms full of strangers.

Monsey

Most Monsey business still moves on who knows who: shul, the neighborhood, an introduction that carries weight. A lot gets done on trust and a phone call. That referral engine is powerful and it is also a ceiling. Sell into the wider Rockland market or out to national buyers and the warm introductions run out. I build the process that replaces them and keep the trust-first way you already sell.

Crown Heights

Crown Heights businesses often have contacts almost everywhere, through the shluchim network and a globally-minded kehilla, and still struggle to convert that reach into steady sales. Warm connections are not a sales process. I build the part that is missing: qualified conversations, follow-up and closed deals, where today there are mostly one-off favors.

Flatbush

A lot of Flatbush business comes from the neighborhood: shul, school and word of mouth across a dense frum community. That base is an asset and a limit. Many serious businesses and professionals here have outgrown referral-only sales, and the founder can no longer be the entire sales team. Selling beyond Flatbush and Midwood, to the wider New York market or national buyers, takes an outbound motion.

Five Towns

In the Five Towns a lot of business moves through a well-connected, professional network across Cedarhurst, Lawrence and Woodmere. Strong relationships open doors, but they are not a sales process. When a Five Towns business has to sell past its own circle or go national, the warm intros run dry. The usual gap is that nobody senior owns the revenue function, and that is the seat I take.

Kiryas Joel

Business in Kiryas Joel runs on community, Yiddish and reputation, inside a very tight village. That is powerful locally and a wall when the buyers are outside, speak English and do not share the same handshake. I have spent years inside the heimishe world and twenty years selling to people who did not know me yet. A KJ business needs both to sell beyond the village.

Passaic

In Passaic and Clifton a lot of business still comes from the community, from word of mouth, and from being close to the New York market. Many owners are sharp, busy, and also the entire sales department. Selling past that base, to regional or national buyers, needs an outbound process the owner does not have to run personally.

Baltimore

Business in Baltimore tends to move through a close, established community: shul, school and long relationships. The businesses are steady and well-run, which is why the next step is so often a proper sales function. I mean a process, a team and a pipeline that take you to regional or national buyers without betting the business on a risky VP hire.

Los Angeles

The frum community in LA is large and spread out, from Pico-Robertson to the Valley, and business often moves through personal and community connections across those pockets. Relationships open doors there too. They stop working when you go after the wider Los Angeles market or national buyers, and that is the point where a business needs a sales motion of its own.

Miami

Miami's frum community is growing fast and much of it is transplanted, so a lot of business still runs on who you knew up north and who you have met since. That network is no substitute for a sales process. Many of these companies have outrun their sales infrastructure, and selling beyond the circle or nationally means building the outbound motion now.

Chicago

In Chicago, centered in West Rogers Park and Peterson Park, business tends to move through a close, established kehilla: shul, school and long relationships. That base is an asset and a ceiling. Selling past the Chicago community, regionally or nationally, needs a process and a senior sales leader to own it, which is the role I fill, hands-on.

Questions frum business owners ask me

Are you really unavailable on Shabbos and Yom Tov?

Yes. No calls, meetings or messages from me on Shabbos or Yom Tov, ever. A sales process that ignores Shabbos, Yom Tov and the zmanim is dead on arrival in our world, so yours gets built around them.

I am in a small kehilla. Who will know I brought you in?

Nobody who does not need to. In a world where everyone knows everyone, your business is your business. I take discretion seriously, and that covers your numbers and the fact that you called me at all.

My customers are all heimish. Can you help me sell to buyers who are not?

Yes, and it is one of the things I do best. Plenty of frum businesses sell well to their own kehilla and freeze when the buyer is secular, mainstream or overseas. I take frum-owned companies into the US mainstream, the EU and beyond. If you are on the other side, an outside company trying to reach frum customers, read how to sell to the frum market.

How does the engagement work?

Hands-on, mostly remote, built around your time zone and the Jewish calendar. It is a monthly retainer with no long contract, and the tiers are public on the pricing page. What past clients say is on the recommendations page. To start, tell me where revenue stalled.

A good name cannot run a sales department

Your reputation took years to earn and it will keep opening doors. But a reputation cannot train a new hire, and it cannot tell you what next quarter looks like. A sales function can. Build one while the name is still strong, and you hand the next salesperson something no stranger can copy: a business people already trust.
