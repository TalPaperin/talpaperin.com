Eight years ago, telling CEOs not to build a sales team sounded insane.

By Tal Paperin· May 17, 2026· 5 min read
B2B SalesHiring & TeamsFractional CRO

At the time, most CEOs thought the idea sounded completely backwards.

Why would a company NOT build its own sales department?

My argument was basically this: most startups and growing companies cannot actually afford a REAL sales infrastructure.

Not a real one.

What a real sales organization costs

Once you calculate the actual cost, it gets ugly fast:

  • VP Sales
  • SDRs
  • AEs
  • managers
  • recruiters
  • onboarding
  • training
  • ramp-up time
  • severance
  • failed hires

Founders budget for the first three lines. Salaries are easy to see. The rest of the list is where the money goes, and almost none of it appears in the plan the board approved.

A recruiter gets paid whether or not the hire works out. A new rep draws a full salary through the months before the first deal closes. Somebody has to train that rep, and in a young company "somebody" is the founder, who is now doing two jobs badly. Then one of the hires turns out to be wrong, and you pay severance, and the recruiter again, and the ramp again.

If you want to see your own number, run it through the sales cost calculator. Most founders are surprised by the total, and more surprised by how little of it is salary.

The cost that kills companies is time

Money you can raise. Time you can't.

One bad VP Sales hire can easily burn 6-12 months.

Here is how those months go. You spend the first stretch searching and interviewing. The VP starts, and reasonably asks for time to learn the product and the market. Then he hires his own people, who need their own ramp. Somewhere in the second half of the year you look at the pipeline and understand it isn't working. By the time you act on that, the year is gone, the runway is shorter, and you are back at the first step with less money and a team that has watched leadership fail.

I know because I was the VP Global Sales & Business Development myself before starting my company. I have been that hire. I know what the job needs around it to succeed, and I know how rarely a company at that stage can provide it.

I kept seeing great products fail to gain momentum because companies tried building enterprise-level sales organizations too early. They copied the org chart of a company ten times their size and expected it to produce that company's revenue. An org chart sells nothing. It costs what it costs, every month, starting on day one.

The model I built instead

So I built a different model.

Instead of building everything from scratch, I step in as a Fractional CRO and we bring the infrastructure with us: trained salespeople, process, execution and systems.

This is exactly what I fix, hands-on. Monthly, no contract, no exit fines. If revenue is stuck, tell me where.

Let's Talk

No endless recruiting. No massive onboarding cycles. No waiting a year for a sales team to become functional.

The logic is simple. The expensive parts of a sales organization are the ones you only need to build once: the hiring profile, the training, the playbook, the management rhythm. A company building its first team pays to invent all of that, makes the beginner's mistakes, and pays again to fix them. A team that already has it arrives working. The client gets senior leadership and people who can sell, and pays for output without funding the experiment.

Where a client has no salespeople at all, that can mean a fully outsourced sales team. Where they have people who underperform, I manage and train the ones already there.

It also changes the risk. A full-time VP is a bet you can't easily reverse. A fractional engagement can be tested small.

That is how it started for us. The client tested us in one market first. Then they expanded us into 5 major Western European countries.

And years later, the VP Sales who managed me wrote publicly on LinkedIn: "Tal is the one you would like to have as your team leader."

That meant a lot to me because he saw the work up close.

How to know if this is you

You probably should not build your own sales department yet if:

  • you have not closed enough deals to say with confidence who buys and why
  • the founder is still the only person who can close
  • you are about to hire a VP Sales mainly because investors expect to see one
  • you could not survive a year of that VP's salary producing nothing
  • nobody in the company has managed salespeople before

In those situations a big hire adds cost before it adds revenue. The guide on when you need a fractional CRO goes through the stages in more detail.

"But I want to own my sales team"

You should, eventually. This is the objection I heard most eight years ago, and it is a fair one. Customer relationships and sales knowledge are assets, and nobody wants them sitting outside the company forever.

The answer is sequence. Prove the motion first, with people who already know how to do it. Learn who buys, what they pay, how long it takes and what a good rep looks like in your market. Then hire your own team into a process that already works, with real numbers to manage them against. Hiring into a working system is a completely different exercise from hiring and hoping.

I never argued against ownership. I argued against building the whole structure before knowing whether it would hold weight.

Today everybody knows the word fractional.

8 years ago, most people thought it was crazy.
