71% of Israeli startups say the war hurt fundraising. 90% are still building.

By Tal Paperin· March 30, 2026· 5 min read
Revenue LeadershipIsraelStartups

And yet they're still here. Still building. Still pushing.

That number is no eulogy.

A survey of 637 Israeli tech companies, published by the Israel Innovation Authority, tells the full story.

Yes, 48% lost a quarter of their workforce to reserve duty. Yes, 87% are dealing with delays. Yes, investors are hesitant.

But only 10% furloughed employees. The other 90% are still fighting, still operating, still finding a way.

This is what Israeli founders are made of. We don't stop. We adapt.

Pressure exposes. It doesn't kill.

Here's what I know from 20+ years of building revenue in hard markets across the world:

Pressure doesn't kill good companies. It exposes weak strategy and forces the right decisions.

In easy times a lot of weakness stays invisible. When money is available, a funding round covers for a sales engine that does not work. Nobody checks whether the pipeline is real, because the bank balance says there is time. Vague positioning survives because buyers have budget for experiments. A sales process that lives in the founder's head works fine, as long as the founder is always available.

Then the round is delayed and a quarter of the team is in uniform. Revenue from customers becomes the only money you control, and every weakness shows at once: the deals that depended on one person, the pipeline that was mostly hope, the customers nobody had called in six months.

The pressure did not create any of this. It was there all along.

Now read the survey numbers one at a time, the way a CEO should.

71% say fundraising was hurt. If outside money is slower, your customer is your investor. That changes the order of work. Collections, renewals, growing existing accounts and deals that can close this quarter come first. The long, prestigious logo that might sign next year comes second.

This is exactly what I fix, hands-on. Monthly, no contract, no exit fines. If revenue is stuck, tell me where.

Let's Talk

48% lost a quarter of their workforce to reserve duty. If the person who left is the one who holds the customer relationships, or the only one who knows how to run the demo, part of your revenue went with him. A sales process that exists only in people's heads cannot survive that. One that is written down can be picked up by someone else on Monday.

87% are dealing with delays. Customers notice delays. They forgive them far more easily when they hear about them from you, early, with a new date attached. Silence loses more accounts than lateness does.

How to tell if your revenue engine is the weak point

Answer honestly:

  • If your best salesperson (or you) were called up tomorrow for a month, would deals keep moving? Who would move them?
  • Can you say today how much cash will arrive from customers in the next 90 days?
  • Do you know which open deals have a real buyer and a real date, and which ones sit in the CRM to keep the total looking healthy?
  • When did someone last speak to each of your ten most important customers about anything other than a support issue?
  • Is your positioning specific enough for a buyer under budget pressure to defend the purchase to his own CFO?

Two answers of "no" or "I don't know," and the revenue engine is where your attention belongs.

What to fix now

The founders who come out of this stronger are the ones using this moment to fix what was always broken: their revenue engine, their positioning, their sales motion.

Not when things get easier. Now.

Here is where to start this week.

  1. Write the sales process down. The stages, what has to be true at each one, the emails, the flow of the demo. Ugly is fine. It has to exist outside one person's head.
  2. Clean the pipeline. Every deal needs a named buyer, a reason to act, and a date that came from the buyer. Everything else comes out. Build your 90-day cash view from what remains. The method is in my guide to sales pipeline and forecasting.
  3. Call your existing customers. All the important ones. Tell them where delivery stands and ask what they need. They are the fastest revenue you have.
  4. Narrow the target. With fewer people you cannot chase every segment. Pick the one where you win fastest and put the whole team on it.
  5. Cover the gaps in writing. For every account whose owner may be called up, name the person who takes over. If nobody inside the company can, bring the capacity in from outside for the period. That is one of the things an outsourced sales team is for.

"We'll deal with sales after the round closes"

I understand the objection. You are short of people, you are tired, and the fundraise eats every spare hour.

But think about what a hesitant investor wants to see. Revenue that grows without new money is the strongest argument you can bring to that meeting, and it is the one argument fully in your hands. If the round slips again, a working revenue engine is also what pays salaries in the meantime.

When the market opens back up

Because when the market opens back up, and it will, the ones who built the infrastructure during the storm will be the ones who scale fastest.

The mechanism is simple. When money returns, many companies get funded around the same time and all of them start hiring salespeople at once. The company that already has a documented process, a clean pipeline and a narrow target drops its new hires into a working machine. The company that waited starts building the machine on investor money, with the clock running.

That is the work I do as a fractional CRO: build a revenue engine that keeps performing when conditions are far from perfect.

90% are still building. Make sure the part you are building is the part that brings in the money.
