Why Your Sales Forecast Is Always Wrong

You commit a number to the board, then miss it by a third. Next quarter, same story. A forecast that is always wrong is not bad luck. It is a pipeline you cannot trust and a process that lets everyone guess.

A forecast is only as honest as the pipeline under it

A forecast is not a spreadsheet exercise. It is a claim about which deals will close and when, and it is only as good as the pipeline it reads from. If the pipeline is full of deals nobody qualified, stages that mean nothing, and probabilities reps set to feel good, the forecast will miss every time. The number on the board is downstream of the discipline in the CRM.

Why the number keeps missing

  • Stages with no documented entry and exit criteria, so a deal is in stage three because a rep felt like it, not because anything happened
  • Reps set their own close probability, so optimism and happy ears inflate the number
  • Deals that have sat untouched for months still counted as live, padding the pipeline
  • A stale CRM nobody updates, so the forecast reads last month's reality
  • Win probabilities that were never calibrated against your actual closed-won history
  • A side spreadsheet that disagrees with the CRM, so there is no single source of truth

What a forecast you can trust actually needs

Defined stages with real exit criteria, so a deal only advances when something concrete happened: a demo done, a champion confirmed, pricing agreed, procurement engaged. Probabilities calibrated to how your deals have actually closed, not round numbers. Weekly deal inspection where someone senior pressure-tests the real deals and calls them honestly. And one source of truth, the CRM, with no shadow spreadsheet running next to it.

How I fix it

I define the stages and the exit criteria, clean the dead weight out of the pipeline, and calibrate probabilities to your real win history. Then I run weekly deal inspection and call the deals honestly, including the ones the team wants to believe in. Within a couple of weeks you have a forecast that holds up, and you can hire, spend and report against it without getting burned.

What a wrong forecast costs you

A forecast you cannot trust poisons every decision that depends on it. You hire ahead of revenue that does not arrive, you commit cash against deals that were never real, and you lose the board's trust in every future number you give them. Once a board stops believing your forecast, every raise and every plan gets harder.

Common questions

Why is my sales forecast always too high?

Almost always because reps set their own probabilities and nobody inspects the deals. Optimism and happy ears inflate the number, dead deals stay in the pipeline, and stages have no real meaning. Fix the stage criteria and run honest weekly inspection and the forecast tightens fast.

Is inaccurate forecasting a CRM problem or a sales problem?

Both, and they feed each other. Undefined stages and uncalibrated probabilities are configuration problems; reps setting their own odds and nobody inspecting deals is a discipline problem. You have to fix the system and the habit together, which is exactly what I do in the first two weeks.

How quickly can you make the forecast reliable?

I define stages and exit criteria and clean the pipeline in the first week, then run weekly deal inspection from there. Within a couple of weeks the forecast reflects real deals, and it keeps tightening as the calibrated probabilities get tested against actual closes.
