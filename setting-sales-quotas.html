How to Set Sales Quotas Reps Can Actually Hit

Most quotas are fiction. The board wants to triple revenue, so the growth number gets divided by the number of reps and handed out as targets. Nobody asked whether a single rep, working a full year, could produce that number. That is not a quota. It is a wish with a name on it. Here is how to set one the team can actually hit.

A quota is a claim about capacity, not a wish

When nobody hits the number, everyone loses. Reps stop believing it, so they stop trying and start managing their excuses instead. You lose the one thing a quota is supposed to give you: a clean signal about who can sell and who cannot. A quota set from fantasy tells you nothing, because everyone misses it.

Build it bottoms-up, not top-down

A real quota starts from what one rep can physically do. Take your actual numbers, not your hopes.

  • Deals per rep per year: how many your best reps actually closed last year, across the full year, not the one good quarter
  • Average deal size: your real ACV weighted toward what closes, not the one whale you signed once
  • Ramp time: a rep hired in Q2 does not carry a full-year number, so prorate it or you are lying to yourself in the forecast

The coverage math behind the number

A quota only means something if there is enough pipeline to feed it. The rule of thumb is three to four times quota in qualified pipeline to have a real shot at the number. Below three times, the math is against the rep no matter how good they are. So a quota is really two numbers: the target, and the pipeline coverage that makes it reachable. Set the target and ignore the coverage and you are back to a wish.

The gap between the board number and reality is data

When the bottoms-up number and the board number do not match, that gap is the most useful thing on the page. It tells you exactly what has to change: more reps, higher ACV, a better win rate, shorter cycles, or a different motion. That is a strategy conversation, not a spreadsheet you paper over by inflating individual targets. Companies that refuse to have it set the impossible number, miss it, blame the reps, churn them, and set the same number again.

Ramp quotas, so new hires are not set up to fail

A new rep carrying a full quota from day one is a rep you are planning to disappoint. Ramp the quota over the first two or three quarters while they learn the motion and build pipeline. A ramp quota protects the hire, gives you an honest read on whether they are tracking, and keeps your forecast from counting revenue that was never going to land that quarter.

Common questions

How do I set a sales quota that is actually achievable?

Build it bottoms-up from one rep's real capacity: deals per rep per year, average deal size and ramp time. Multiply deals by deal size, subtract for ramp, and you have the honest floor. Set the quota above it so it stretches, but within reach of a good rep having a good year.

How much pipeline do I need to hit quota?

A common benchmark is three to four times quota in qualified pipeline. Below three times, the math is against the rep regardless of how good they are. A quota is really two numbers: the target and the coverage that makes it reachable.

What do I do when the board number is higher than capacity?

Treat the gap as data, not a rounding error. It tells you what has to change: more reps, higher ACV, a better win rate or a different motion. That is a strategy decision, not something you fix by inflating each rep's target and hoping.

Should new reps carry a full quota?

No. Ramp the quota over the first two or three quarters while they learn the motion and build pipeline. A full number from day one sets the hire up to fail and puts revenue in your forecast that was never going to land.
